# Raw research — starting solids

> Verbatim findings from three parallel research agents (2026-08-05). Source URLs, effect sizes, and explicit "could not verify" flags preserved as returned. Synthesis lives in [FEEDING.md](FEEDING.md).

## ALLERGEN INTRODUCTION

## BOTTOM LINE ON THE BELIEF

"Avoid allergens first, then slowly introduce" is the **exact recommendation that was withdrawn** and is now contradicted by high-certainty RCT evidence. No current guideline from any major body (NIAID, AAP, AAAAI/ACAAI/CSACI, EAACI, ESPGHAN, ASCIA, BSACI) recommends delay for any allergen. Delay is the risk factor, not the safeguard.

**Origin of the belief:** AAP 2000 advised high-risk infants delay cow's milk to age 1, egg to 2, peanut/tree nuts/fish to age 3 — while acknowledging there were no conclusive studies behind it. AAP partially reversed in 2008 ("should not be delayed"), fully in 2019. https://www.contemporarypediatrics.com/view/new-evidence-changes-guidelines-food-allergies | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6157280/

Fear-driven avoidance was widespread: in the 2010 UK Infant Feeding Survey, **45% of mothers of 8–10-month-olds avoided a food** — 48% nuts, 14% eggs, 10% dairy, 6% fish; "fear of allergy" was the top reason (EAT paper intro, NEJM 2016). https://openaccess.sgul.ac.uk/id/eprint/107787/7/nejmoa1514210.pdf

## 1. THE LANDMARK TRIALS — ACTUAL NUMBERS

### LEAP (Du Toit et al., NEJM 2015; 372:803-13)
- **n = 640** infants, **4–11 months**, with **severe eczema, egg allergy, or both**. Stratified into two cohorts by baseline peanut SPT: negative, and 1–4 mm wheal. Infants with SPT >4 mm were **excluded** (76 screened out).
- **Intervention:** ≥6 g peanut protein/week across **≥3 meals/week** (≈2 g protein × 3), sustained to **60 months**. Bamba (peanut puff) was the vehicle.
- **SPT-negative cohort (n = 530, ITT): 13.7% avoidance vs 1.9% consumption, P<0.001** → **12.6 percentage-point absolute risk reduction, 86.1% relative risk reduction, NNT 8.5**
- **SPT-positive cohort (n = 98): 35.3% vs 10.6%, P = 0.004** → **24.7 pp ARR, 70% RRR, NNT 4**
- **Combined ITT: 17.2% vs 3.2%** → 14 pp ARR, ~80% RRR, NNT ~7.1
- Immunology: consumption group had higher peanut sIgG4; avoidance group higher IgE. Larger SPT wheal + lower IgG4:IgE ratio predicted allergy.
- **Safety at baseline OFC:** of 272 infants with a 0 mm wheal challenged, **1 reaction** (urticarial rash, treated with chlorpheniramine). Of 29 with 1–2 mm wheal, **2 mild reactions**, no epinephrine.
- Numbers verbatim from the NIAID addendum (which quotes LEAP): https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/Media-Library-PDFs/Allergist%20Resources/Statements%20and%20Practice%20Parameters/Addendum-guidelines-for-the-prevention-of-peanut-allergy-in-the-United-States.pdf | abstract: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25705822/ | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4685572/

### LEAP-On (Du Toit et al., NEJM 2016) — does tolerance stick?
- **556 enrolled** (88.5% of 628 eligible); 550 with complete data. All participants **avoided peanut for 12 months, ages 60→72 months**.
- **At 72 months: 18.6% (52/280) in the original-avoidance group vs 4.8% (13/270) in the original-consumption group, P<0.001.**
- Adherence to the 12-month avoidance: 90.4% (avoidance arm) vs 69.3% (consumption arm).
- **Conclusion: 12 months off peanut did NOT increase peanut allergy — the tolerance was durable, not merely suppression.** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26942922/

### EAT (Perkin et al., NEJM 2016; 374:1733-43) — the "negative" trial that actually supports early introduction
- **n = 1303** exclusively breastfed infants recruited at **3 months** from the **general population** (England & Wales) — not high-risk.
- Six foods, introduced in this order: **cow's milk yogurt first, then in random order peanut, cooked (boiled) hen's egg, sesame, whitefish — wheat always last.** Target **2 g of allergen protein twice weekly, per food**.
- **ITT primary outcome (food allergy to ≥1 of 6 foods, ages 1–3 y): 7.1% (42/595) standard vs 5.6% (32/567) early, P = 0.32 — not significant.**
- ITT per food: peanut 2.5% vs 1.2% (51% lower RR, P = 0.11); egg 5.4% vs 3.7% (31% lower, P = 0.17).
- **Per-protocol: any food allergy 7.3% vs 2.4%, P = 0.01; peanut 2.5% vs 0%, P = 0.003; egg 5.5% vs 1.4%, P = 0.009.** No significant effect for milk, sesame, fish, or wheat.
- **The ITT null is an adherence artifact:** only ~42–43% of evaluable early-introduction infants hit the per-protocol target (34% of all early-introduction infants); non-white participants 13.3%. Eating 2 g/week of peanut or egg-white protein was associated with significantly lower allergy than eating less — **a dose-response, not a null effect.**
- **EAT high-risk subgroup analysis (JACI 2019):** among infants sensitized at enrollment (≥0.1 kU/L), food allergy **34.2% → 19.2%, P = .03**; egg-sensitized infants, egg allergy **48.6% → 20.0%, P = .01**. Among infants with moderate eczema (SCORAD 15–<40): any food allergy **46.7% → 22.6%, P = .048**; egg allergy **43.3% → 16.1%, P = .02**.
- https://openaccess.sgul.ac.uk/id/eprint/107787/7/nejmoa1514210.pdf | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6902243/

### PETIT (Natsume et al., Lancet 2017; 389:276-86) — egg, Japan
- **n = 147** Japanese infants **with eczema**, randomized **at 4–5 months**, double-blind placebo-controlled.
- **Stepwise heated egg powder: 50 mg/day from 6–9 months, then 250 mg/day from 9–12 months.** Eczema aggressively treated and kept controlled in **both** arms.
- **Egg allergy at 12 months: 8% (5/60) egg group vs 38% (23/61) placebo. RR 0.221 (95% CI 0.090–0.543), P = 0.0001.**
- **Trial stopped early** at scheduled interim analysis. Safety: 6 hospitalizations in the egg arm vs 0 placebo (P = 0.022); 19 acute events in 9 (15%) egg vs 14 events in 11 (18%) placebo.
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27939035/

### Critical contrast — HEAP (raw/pasteurized egg)
The German HEAP trial and the STAR trial used **raw pasteurized egg powder** and had to be halted / had major safety problems: in STAR, **one-third of subjects reacted (including anaphylaxis and FPIES) on initial exposure**; in HEAP, of 23 excluded for pre-existing egg sIgE, 17 were challenged and **16 reacted**. **Form matters: well-cooked egg only.** (AAAAI/ACAAI/CSACI 2021 consensus, p.29)

### Meta-analyses
- **Ierodiakonou et al., JAMA 2016;316:1181-92:** egg introduced at 4–6 months, **RR 0.56 (0.36–0.87)**, 5 trials/1915 participants, moderate certainty. Peanut at 4–11 months, **RR 0.29 (0.11–0.74)**, 2 trials/1550 participants, moderate certainty. https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2553447
- **Scarpone et al., JAMA Pediatrics 2023;177:489-97** (the current basis for ASCIA 2026): egg introduced 3–6 months, **RR 0.60 (0.46–0.77), I²=0%, 9 trials/4811 participants — HIGH certainty (GRADE)**. Peanut introduced 3–10 months, **RR 0.31 (0.19–0.51), I²=21%, 4 trials/3796 — HIGH certainty**. Multiple-allergen introduction 2–12 months: any food allergy **RR 0.49 (0.33–0.74)**, 4 trials/3295. Counterweight: **withdrawal from trials RR 2.29 (1.45–3.63)** — feeding large volumes of allergen before 6 months is hard on infants. (via ASCIA 2026 guideline, §3.2.2–3.2.4)

## 2. WHAT THE GUIDELINES ACTUALLY SAY

### NIAID 2017 Addendum (Togias et al., JACI 2017;139:29-44) — verbatim
> **Addendum guideline 1:** "infants with **severe eczema, egg allergy, or both** have introduction of age-appropriate peanut-containing food **as early as 4 to 6 months of age**... **Other solid foods should be introduced before peanut-containing foods to show that the infant is developmentally ready.** ...evaluation with peanut-specific IgE measurement, SPTs, or both be **strongly considered** before introduction."
>
> **Addendum guideline 2:** "infants with **mild-to-moderate eczema** should have introduction of age-appropriate peanut-containing food **around 6 months of age**, in accordance with family preferences and cultural practices... may have dietary peanut introduced **at home without an in-office evaluation**."
>
> **Addendum guideline 3:** "infants **without eczema or any food allergy** have age-appropriate peanut-containing foods **freely introduced** in the diet together with other solid foods and in accordance with family preferences and cultural practices." (Table I: "Age appropriate and in accordance with family preferences and cultural practices")

Definitions (verbatim): **Severe eczema** = "persistent or frequently recurring eczema... assessed as severe by a health care provider and requiring frequent need for prescription-strength topical corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, or other anti-inflammatory agents despite appropriate use of emollients." **Egg allergy** = "a history of an allergic reaction to egg and a skin prick test wheal diameter of 3 mm or greater with egg white extract, or a positive oral egg food challenge result."

**Maintenance dose (Appendix F, verbatim):** "the total amount of peanut protein to be regularly consumed per week should be **approximately 6 to 7 g over 3 or more feedings**."

### AAP 2019 Clinical Report (Greer et al., Pediatrics 2019;143:e20190281)
- "**There is no evidence that delaying the introduction of allergenic foods, including peanuts, eggs, and fish, beyond 4 to 6 months prevents atopic disease.**"
- "There is now evidence that early introduction of peanuts may prevent peanut allergy."
- "Current evidence does not support a role for maternal dietary restrictions during pregnancy or lactation."
- "There is a lack of evidence that partially or extensively hydrolyzed formula prevents atopic disease."
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30886111/

### AAAAI / ACAAI / CSACI 2021 Consensus (Fleischer et al., JACI:In Practice 2021;9:22-43) — verbatim recommendations
- **Rec 1:** severe eczema = highest risk; mild-moderate eczema, parental atopy, or one existing food allergy = some increased risk; "**food allergy often develops in infants who have no identifiable risk factors.**" No clear evidence that a sibling of a peanut-allergic child is at increased risk — "though such infants **may be at risk of developing peanut allergy secondary to delayed introduction of peanut.**"
- **Rec 2 (peanut, Strong/A):** "Introduce peanut-containing products to **all infants, irrespective of their relative risk**, starting **around 6 months of life, though not before 4 months**... **screening peanut skin or sIgE testing and/or in-office introduction is not required**... Once peanut is introduced, **regular ingestion should be maintained.**"
- **Rec 3 (egg, Strong/A):** same timing; "**Use only cooked forms of egg and avoid administering any raw, pasteurized egg-containing products.**"
- **Rec 4 (all other allergens, moderate):** "**Do not deliberately delay** the introduction of other potentially allergenic complementary foods (CM, soy, wheat, tree nuts, sesame, fish, shellfish)... There **may be potential harm in delaying**."
- **Rec 5:** feed a diverse diet.
- **Rec 6:** do not prescribe hydrolyzed formula for food allergy prevention.
- Maternal exclusion of allergens in pregnancy/lactation **not recommended**.
- PDF: https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/MediaLibrary/PDF%20Documents/Practice%20and%20Parameters/A-Consensus-Approach-to-the-Primary-Prevention-of-Food-Allergy-Through-Nutrition-Jan-21.pdf

### ASCIA 2026 (Vale et al., Clin Exp Allergy 2026, doi:10.1111/cea.70217) — the newest guideline, 16 recommendations
Replaces the 2016 ASCIA guideline; systematic review + AGREE II + Delphi. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cea.70217 | full text PDF: https://www.ifan.ie/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Clin-Experimental-Allergy-2026-Vale-ASCIA-Guideline-Infant-Feeding-for-Food-Allergy-Prevention.pdf
1. Solids when developmentally ready — "usually around 6 months and not before 4 months." (Moderate)
2. **"When introducing new common food allergens, only introduce ONE new allergen at the same meal."** → "**Commercial allergen introduction products containing several food allergens in the same serve are not recommended.**" (95% consensus)
3. **Well-cooked egg soon after starting solids** — irrespective of family/personal allergy history. (Moderate)
4. **Peanut, age-appropriate form, soon after starting solids** — irrespective of risk. (Moderate)
5. Other allergens (**cow's milk, wheat, tree nuts, sesame, soy, fish, shellfish**) in the **first year**, prioritising the family's usual diet. (Very low certainty)
6. **"If there is an allergy to a specific food, other common food allergens should still be introduced."** (92%)
7. **Mild perioral rash alone (redness/contact urticaria, no other symptoms) may not be allergy — try the food again.** Barrier cream before feeding helps. (83%)
8. **"Food should not be smeared onto the skin to test if an infant is allergic"** — skin contact without eating may itself be a sensitisation risk. (83%)
9–11. **Once introduced, offer each allergen — including egg and peanut — at least ONCE A WEEK.** (91–96%)
12. No maternal elimination while breastfeeding. 13–15. Hydrolysed / soy / goat/sheep/rice/oat/pea/coconut formulas not recommended for prevention. 16. Avoid transient cow's-milk-formula top-ups in the first week of life in healthy breastfed infants unless medically needed.

Notable: ASCIA explicitly says evidence supports peanut and egg introduction **around 4–6 months rather than earlier or later**, citing a post-hoc LEAP+EAT model showing "prevention decreased with increasing age at introduction, from 4 to 12 months."

### EAACI 2021 (Halken et al., Pediatr Allergy Immunol 2021;32:843-58)
Introduce **well-cooked (not raw/uncooked pasteurized) egg** as part of complementary feeding; introduce **peanut in age-appropriate form** in populations with high peanut allergy prevalence; **avoid regular cow's milk formula as supplementary feed in the first week of life**. Most effective window for egg and peanut: **4–6 months**. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/pai.13496

### ESPGHAN 2017 (Fewtrell et al., JPGN 2017;64:119-32)
Complementary foods **not before 4 months, not delayed beyond 6 months**. **Allergenic foods can be started any time after 4 months.** High-risk infants (severe eczema and/or egg allergy): peanut between **4 and 11 months**, after specialist evaluation. **Gluten any time between 4 and 12 months** — timing does not affect celiac risk. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1097/MPG.0000000000001454

### BSACI / BDA (UK)
"**The deliberate exclusion or delayed introduction of specific allergenic foods MAY INCREASE the risk of developing a food allergy to the same foods.**" Solids around 6 months (not before 4); include egg, peanut, tree nuts, pasteurised dairy, fish/seafood, wheat; **aim to introduce these before 12 months, one new food at a time**; continue regularly. Higher-risk infants (early-onset or moderate-severe eczema, or existing food allergy) may benefit from cooked egg then peanut **from 4 months**. https://www.bsaci.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/pdf_Early-feeding-guidance-for-HCPs-2.pdf

**No guideline reviewed recommends delay for any allergen.**

## 3. MECHANISM — DUAL-ALLERGEN EXPOSURE HYPOTHESIS (Lack, JACI 2008;121:1331-6)

- **Sensitization via skin; tolerance via gut.** Low-dose environmental food protein (tabletops, hands, house dust, caregivers' hands) penetrates a defective skin barrier, is taken up by Langerhans cells → **Th2 response → IgE**. Early **high-dose oral** consumption drives **Th1 and regulatory T-cell responses in gut-associated lymphoid tissue → tolerance**.
- **This is why delay backfires:** while you're "avoiding" the food orally, the baby is still meeting it on the skin — you get all the sensitizing exposure and none of the tolerizing exposure. Avoidance is not zero exposure; it's skin-only exposure.
- Explains the eczema link: severe/early eczema = broken barrier = the sensitizing route is wide open. Filaggrin loss-of-function mutations link eczema severity and food allergy. Meta-analysis: infants with eczema have **OR 6.18 (95% CI 2.94–12.98)** for food sensitization in population-based studies; in eczema-only cohorts, food sensitization up to 66% and challenge-proven food allergy up to 81% (AAAAI 2021, Table III).
- EAT measured **peanut protein in the infant's bed dust at 3 months** — environmental exposure was a study variable, not a hypothetical.
- Practical corollaries now in guidelines: **ASCIA Rec 8** (don't smear food on skin to "test"; frequent skin contact without eating may be a sensitisation risk) and **ASCIA Rec 5 practical implications** ("Environmental exposure to food allergens in the household without being eaten by the infant may be a risk factor for development of allergy").
- https://www.jacionline.org/article/S0091-6749(08)00778-1/fulltext | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11471915/

## 4. PRACTICAL PROTOCOL (from the guidelines/trials)

**When to start:** solids around **6 months**, not before **4 months**, when developmentally ready (sits supported, holds head steady, swallows rather than spits, coordinates eye-hand-mouth, interested in others eating). Most infants ready between 5 and 7 months (ASCIA).

**Order:** **Peanut and egg should NOT be the first food.** AAAAI 2021: "Neither peanut nor egg should be the first food offered... because it may be difficult to distinguish between an inability to manage complementary food, which may result in gagging, vomiting, or coughing... these symptoms may be incorrectly interpreted as an allergic reaction." Start with a few tolerated purees (fruit/veg/iron-fortified cereal), which then double as the vehicle for mixing in peanut. Same in NIAID G1/G2 verbatim.

**One at a time, but no mandatory waiting period.** ASCIA Rec 2: only one NEW allergen per meal. AAAAI Appendix A Q9 is explicit: "Introducing new allergenic foods one at a time over a 3-day period is a common recommendation (**that fits a more common sense than evidence-based approach**)... **There is no specific order in which the other allergens should be introduced, and no specific wait time between new foods that is required or shown to work.**" BSACI: "leaving at least a day between them." EAT introduced wheat last by design.

**First feeding at home (NIAID Appendix D, verbatim protocol):**
1. Only when the infant is **healthy** — no cold, vomiting, diarrhea.
2. **At home**, not daycare or a restaurant.
3. At least **one adult free of distractions**.
4. **Set aside 2 hours** afterward to watch.
5. Offer a **small amount on the tip of a spoon → wait 10 minutes → if no reaction, give the rest at the infant's usual pace.**

**Serving amounts:**
| Source | Amount |
|---|---|
| LEAP | ≥6 g peanut protein/week over ≥3 meals (≈2 g × 3) until age 5; median actual intake 7.7 g/wk (IQR 6.7–8.8) |
| NIAID maintenance | ~**6–7 g peanut protein/week over ≥3 feedings** |
| NIAID first dose | ~**2 g peanut protein** cumulative |
| PETIT (egg) | 50 mg/day heated egg powder 6–9 mo; 250 mg/day 9–12 mo |
| EAT | 2 g protein **twice weekly** per food, 6 foods |
| AAAAI 2021 (pragmatic) | "**1 to 2 teaspoons of peanut butter or egg... at least once weekly**, and larger amounts if the child enjoys the food" |
| ASCIA 2026 | **at least once a week**, each allergen |

**2 g peanut protein = any ONE of** (NIAID Appendix D): 21 pieces / ~17 g Bamba (≈⅔ bag); **2 level tsp (9–10 g) smooth peanut butter**; 8 g ground peanuts (2½ level tsp); 2 level tsp (4 g) peanut flour or peanut butter powder.

**AAAAI's honest caveat on dosing:** "there is **no known dose relationship** to either peanut allergy or egg allergy outcome... **Regular exposure for several years is felt to be more important than focusing on a particular fixed dosing interval or amount.**" And the LEAP dose was reverse-engineered from Israeli children's median monthly intake (7.1 g), recalled by parents years after — "an approximate target to achieve, rather than a rigid requirement."

**Keep it in rotation — do not introduce and drop.** ASCIA Recs 9–11: at least once weekly, indefinitely. AAAAI: "once peanut has been introduced into the infant's diet, **consumption should not be stopped unless there are signs/symptoms of an allergic reaction**." ASCIA: "Introducing a food allergen and then not offering it regularly may be associated with the development of food allergy." LEAP-On shows a *deliberate 12-month gap after 5 years of consumption* was safe — that is **not** license to stop at 8 months old.

**What a reaction looks like (NIAID Appendix D + AAAAI Table E2):**
- *Mild:* a new rash, or a few hives around the mouth/face.
- *More severe (any of):* lip swelling, vomiting, widespread hives over the body, face or tongue swelling, difficulty breathing, wheeze, repetitive coughing, colour change (pale, blue), sudden lethargy/limpness. → **immediate medical attention / call emergency services.**
- By organ system (AAAAI Table E2): skin/subcutaneous (systemic urticaria, angioedema, flushing — **most common in infants**); upper airway (rhinorrhea, sneezing, congestion); lower airway (cough, wheeze, stridor, retractions); GI (**persistent profuse vomiting**, diarrhea); CV (tachycardia, hypotension); CNS (**behavioral change — irritability, inconsolable crying, clinging, lethargy**).
- Timing: IgE-mediated symptoms usually **within 30 minutes** (BSACI); NIAID says contact the provider if mild symptoms occur **within 2 hours** of eating.

**What is NOT a reaction:**
- **ASCIA Rec 7:** mild perioral rash (redness or contact urticaria) during/right after eating, with no other symptoms, **may not be allergy — offer the food again.** Use barrier cream before feeding, especially with perioral eczema. Removing a food on a false alarm may itself increase allergy risk.
- Gagging, coughing, or vomiting from **texture inexperience** is commonly mistaken for allergy (AAAAI) — another reason peanut/egg go after a few starter foods.
- AAAAI notes some LEAP infants reacted at home after tolerating introduction, and some of those turned out **not** to be peanut-allergic — urticaria from a concurrent viral infection, or contact reaction to acidic fruit purée used as the mixing vehicle. Referral should be **within days**, not weeks/months, to avoid unnecessary elimination.
- BSACI risk framing: severe reaction risk estimated at **1–2 per 1000 higher-risk babies**; "**no life-threatening reactions to date have been reported as a result of earlier introduction**" (anaphylaxis has been reported with **raw egg powder**).

**FPIES basics (non-IgE — a different thing entirely):**
- **Delayed, forceful, repetitive/projectile vomiting 1–4 hours** after ingestion; pallor and lethargy; diarrhea may follow at 5–10 hours. No hives, no wheeze — it does not look like classic allergy and **antihistamines/epinephrine are not the mainstay** (fluids ± ondansetron).
- **Most common triggers: cow's milk (most common worldwide), soy, rice, oat** — i.e. the "bland safe first foods." Combined rice/oat FPIES in ~⅓ of rice-FPIES cases in the US and Australia.
- 2017 International Consensus Guidelines (Nowak-Węgrzyn et al., JACI): https://www.aaaai.org/Aaaai/media/Media-Library-PDFs/Tools%20for%20the%20Public/Conditions%20Library/Library%20-%20Allergies/April-2017-Guidelines-FPIES-Diagnosis-Management.pdf | https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5804009/
- Prognosis good: most tolerate the trigger by **3–5 years**.
- Also distinguish **delayed non-IgE food allergy** (BSACI): symptoms hours-to-days later, recurrent abdominal pain, worsening vomiting/reflux, feeding difficulty, loose stools >6–8×/day or constipation ≤2×/week, skin reddening/itching/worsening eczema. **Cannot cause anaphylaxis.**

## 5. THE HIGH-RISK CAVEAT — WHO SEES A DOCTOR FIRST

**Per NIAID 2017, testing before peanut is "strongly considered" ONLY for Guideline 1 infants: severe eczema, egg allergy, or both** (definitions in §2 above). Guidelines 2 and 3 — mild/moderate eczema and no eczema — **introduce at home, no testing.**

**Testing pathway (NIAID Fig 1):**
- **Peanut sIgE <0.35 kU_A/L** → risk of reaction low, >90% will have a negative SPT → introduce at home OR supervised in office, provider/parent preference.
- **Peanut sIgE ≥0.35** → refer to specialist for consultation/SPT.
- **SPT wheal 0–2 mm** above saline → low risk (95% will not have peanut allergy) → home introduction or in-office supervised feeding.
- **SPT wheal 3–7 mm** → moderate-to-high risk → **supervised feeding in office OR graded OFC in a specialized facility.** Note: many of these infants are sensitized but not allergic and **stand to benefit most** from early introduction.
- **SPT wheal ≥8 mm** → probably allergic → ongoing specialist evaluation and management.
- **Do NOT run food allergen panels or sIgE for foods other than peanut** — poor positive predictive value, leads to overdiagnosis and unnecessary dietary restriction (NIAID; echoed by AAAAI + Choosing Wisely).
- In-office supervised feeding protocol: tip-of-teaspoon → wait 10 min → feed remainder at usual pace → **observe 30 min after the full 2 g**. Graded OFC = 5 incremental doses 15–20 min apart, cumulative ~4 g peanut protein.

**But note the divergence — this is the single biggest inter-guideline disagreement:**
- **AAAAI/ACAAI/CSACI 2021, ASCIA, EAACI, BSACI, and Israeli practice all say screening is NOT required**, even for high-risk infants. AAAAI: "While screening peanut skin or sIgE testing and/or in-office introduction is not required for early introduction, this remains an option to consider for families that prefer to not introduce peanut at home; this decision is **preference-sensitive**."
- **Cost-effectiveness (AAAAI Table VI, 20-year Markov model, per at-risk patient with early-onset eczema and/or egg allergy):** no screening + early introduction = $6,557, 19.63 QALY, 0.40 reactions. SPT screening first = $7,576, 19.62 QALY (incremental societal cost **$654,115,322**). sIgE screening = $7,977, 19.60 QALY (**$911,211,774**). **Delayed introduction = $11,708, 19.46 QALY, 0.72 reactions — worst on every axis.** For egg (early-onset eczema): no screening $2,235/19.78 QALY/0.03 reactions vs delayed cooked introduction $10,615/19.53/0.13.
- **BSACI:** "up to **50% of positive tests in this age group are false positives**"; test only where there is prompt access to supervised food challenge; testing should **not delay** introduction beyond 12 months. Population screening of higher-risk infants would require testing ~16% of all infants, catch ~¾ of peanut allergy cases, and generate food challenges in 15–30% of those screened.
- **Siblings of food-allergic children:** AAAAI Rec 1 and BSACI both say sibling status alone does **not** significantly raise risk — and BSACI adds that parents in this situation tend to delay, "**it is this which seems to increase the risk.**"
- **Eczema control first:** BSACI — eczema should be "well-controlled i.e. cleared prior to introducing allergenic foods." Refer if eczema stays uncontrolled, needs >1% hydrocortisone daily for >6 weeks, or needs a more potent steroid more than once. PETIT's design point: aggressive eczema treatment was applied to **both** arms — early egg was tested *on top of* good eczema control, not instead of it. ASCIA Rec 3: the recommendation applies to eczema infants too — "Eczema should be well managed."
- **Baseline prevalence matters (BSACI absolute numbers):** general population — ~1 in 200 infants is **already** egg-allergic and ~1 in 200 already peanut-allergic before 6 months. Moderate-severe eczema — **up to 30% already egg-allergic**, up to 10% already peanut-allergic before 6 months. This is the honest case for a clinician conversation in that specific group, not for delay.

## 6. PER-ALLERGEN SPECIFICS + CHOKING-SAFE FORMS

| Allergen | Evidence on timing | Safe form |
|---|---|---|
| **Peanut** | High certainty, RR 0.31 (0.19–0.51). 4–6 mo optimal (ASCIA/EAACI); ~6 mo, not before 4 (AAAAI/NIAID G2-3); 4–6 mo for high-risk (NIAID G1) | **Never whole nuts (<5 y per NIAID; <4 y per AAAAI). Never peanut butter from a spoon or in lumps/dollops (<4 y).** Thin 2 tsp PB with 2–3 tsp hot water, stir, cool. Or mix 2 tsp PB into 2–3 Tbsp tolerated purée. Or 2 tsp peanut flour/PB powder into ~2 Tbsp purée. Bamba/peanut puffs: 21 pieces; **soften with 4–6 tsp water if <7 months.** AAAAI prefers thinned natural peanut butter or peanut flour long-term over puff snacks |
| **Egg** | High certainty, RR 0.60 (0.46–0.77). Soon after starting solids | **Well-cooked ONLY** — boiled, scrambled, or baked into muffin/pancake/teething biscuit. **Never raw or raw pasteurized egg powder** (anaphylaxis + FPIES in STAR/HEAP). Raw egg also carries infection risk. Mash with vegetables for texture. AAAAI: undercooked egg not recommended <1 year |
| **Cow's milk** | **No evidence early introduction prevents CMA** — 6 trials/3900, RR 0.84 (0.38–1.87), **very low** certainty. But: avoid transient CM-formula top-ups in the **first week of life** in healthy breastfed infants (ASCIA Rec 16, EAACI; low certainty, single trial). Do not delay | Yogurt, cheese (from ~6–7 mo), milk baked into foods. **Liquid cow's milk as a main drink not before 12 months — for non-allergy reasons: renal solute load, low iron** |
| **Wheat** | 3 trials/3169, **RR 0.66 (0.10–4.47) — NS**. ESPGHAN: gluten any time 4–12 mo; timing does not change celiac risk. Do not delay | Softly cooked pasta (good finger food), bread fingers, wheat in baked goods. EAT introduced wheat last |
| **Soy** | Insufficient data for meta-analysis. Soy formula **not** recommended for prevention (very low certainty). Do not delay | Tofu, soy yogurt, soy milk in food. **Avoid soy sauce** — little protein, very high salt |
| **Sesame** | **Not examined** in Scarpone 2023; a 2019 SR found only limited evidence. EAT included it; no significant per-protocol effect. Do not delay | Hummus or tahini **thinned/mixed into purée**. Sesame candy only for older children |
| **Fish / shellfish** | Insufficient data for meta-analysis. Diet-diversity data: fish in the first year associated with reduced food allergy prevalence to age 6 (Roduit). Do not delay | A few portions of the species the family actually eats, then keep it regular. **No more than 2 portions of oily fish per week** (FDA/FSA). Deboned, flaked |
| **Tree nuts** | Insufficient data. Do not delay | **Shelled/whole tree nuts not before age 4** (choking) — **tree nut butters** are the safe alternative, thinned. Choose nuts reflecting the family's cuisine (cashew, walnut); unclear whether specific nuts matter |

**General choking rules (ASCIA "safe food practices"):** under 4 years, only food they can chew and swallow with ease, always supervised. **Avoid hard foods: nuts (whole OR in pieces), raw vegetables, whole grapes.** Also avoid raw eggs and honey in infancy (bacterial risk).

**Do not use multi-allergen commercial powders** if you want to be able to identify a trigger — ASCIA Rec 2 explicitly names them as not recommended.

# IRON & FIRST FOODS AT ~6 MONTHS — RAW RESEARCH FINDINGS (Philippines context)

## 1. WHY IRON AT 6 MONTHS

**The requirement number (the famous one)**
- RDA for infants **7–12 months = 11 mg/day** elemental iron. AAP 2010 clinical report; also the IOM/NIH DRI value. Confirmed independently by CPS ("For infants 7 to 12 months of age, 11 mg/day of elemental iron") — https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/iron-requirements — and AAFP's summary of the AAP report ("Iron intake in infants between six and 12 months of age should be 11 mg per day"; 1–3 years = 7 mg/day) — https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2011/0301/p624.html
- Underlying math: **EAR 6.9 mg/day**; the actual *physiologically absorbed* requirement is only **~0.69 mg/day**, i.e. the 11 mg figure assumes **~10% absorption** — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8707490/
- Context: 11 mg/day is *higher than the adult male RDA (8 mg)*. This is the single most striking framing number.
- AAP primary URL 403s to automated fetch (https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/126/5/1040/65343/) — figures above are from CPS + AAFP restatements, both explicit.

**Fetal store depletion**
- CPS: "Most healthy term infants of normal birth weight are born with sufficient stores to meet their iron requirements... until they are 6 months old. At about this time, iron stores become depleted." — https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/iron-requirements
- ABM Protocol #29: fetal iron supplies become diminished during "the second half of the first year"; breast milk contains **~0.3 mg/L** iron — https://abm.memberclicks.net/assets/DOCUMENTS/PROTOCOLS/29-iron-zinc-and-vitamin-d-protocol-english.pdf
- **Conflict to note:** CPS states human-milk iron absorption is "20% to 50%... compared with 10% to 20% from infant formulas"; the PMC8707490 review states breast milk 10–20% and formula 20–50%. The *direction* (breast milk iron is more bioavailable but far lower in quantity) is the consistent claim; the exact percentages disagree across sources — treat specific % as soft.
- AAP/AAFP: exclusively breastfed term infants should get **1 mg/kg/day supplemental iron from 4 months** until iron-rich complementary foods are established. Preterm: 2 mg/kg/day from 1 month.

**How big the gap actually is (best quantitative anchor found)**
- J Pediatr 2021 (FITS 2016 dietary database): calculated daily absorbed iron was below the physiologic requirement in **54.3% of all 6–12 month infants** — **95.8% of 296 exclusively breastfed**, 72.2% of 102 mixed-fed, 19.5% of 448 exclusively formula-fed. Mean calculated absorption in 6–9 month breastfed infants was **0.27 mg/day** vs the ~0.69 mg/day requirement. — https://www.jpeds.com/article/S0022-3476(20)31370-6/fulltext (full text 403s; figures from indexed abstract/summary — worth re-verifying the 0.27 figure if load-bearing)

**Neurodevelopment evidence (Lozoff)**
- **Lozoff 1991, NEJM** (Costa Rica, 5-year follow-up, 163 of 191 children retained): children with moderate IDA in infancy (Hb ≤100 g/L) scored lower at age 5 after controlling for SES. Woodcock-Johnson preschool cluster **448.6 ±9.7 (n=30, anemic) vs 452.9 ±9.2 (n=133), P<0.01**; visual-motor integration **5.9 ±2.1 vs 6.7 ±2.3, P<0.05**. All had normal Hb by age 5. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1870641/ / https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM199109053251004
- **Lozoff 2000, Pediatrics** (same cohort at 11–14y, mean 12.3y; 48 with chronic severe ID vs 114 good iron status): persistent significant deficits in arithmetic achievement, written expression, motor functioning, spatial memory, selective recall, tachistoscopic threshold. More grade repetition / special services referral. Parents and teachers rated more anxiety/depression, social problems, attention problems. Conclusion: severe chronic ID in infancy identifies children "at developmental and behavioral risk >10 years after iron treatment." — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10742372/
- Lozoff 2006 review (Nutrition Reviews) — long-lasting neural + behavioral effects; iron therapy started at diagnosis of IDA does not fully correct earlier iron-deficiency-mediated brain dysfunction. — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1753-4887.2006.tb00243.x
- Altered neural correlates of recognition memory still detectable at **10 years** — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022347611012613
- A **"8–9 points lower cognitive scores, persisting up to 19 years"** figure appeared in search summaries attributed to Pediatrics 2022 (https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/150/6/e2021055926/190098/) — **could not verify** the exact magnitude against the primary text; do not quote as a number without checking.
- **Important counterweight for honesty:** supplementation trials show only modest reversal. Meta-analysis of 3 RCTs (n=561) found no significant effect of iron supplementation on Mental Development Index at 12 months; benefits are clearest in already-anemic/iron-deficient subjects with ≥2 months supplementation. 12 of 14 studies had high/moderate risk of bias. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22142898/ , https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15877905/ , Cochrane: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23744449/ → the prevention-over-rescue framing is the evidence-supported one.
- WHO anemia definition for reference: Hb **<110 g/L** in children 6–59 months. Note recent Lancet Haematol re-analysis puts the 5th centile at **104.4 g/L for 6–23 months** — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10983828/

## 2. WHAT GUIDELINES SAY ABOUT IRON-RICH *FIRST* FOODS

**WHO 2023 guideline (complementary feeding, 6–23 months)** — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK596423/ and https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK596430/
- Rec 3: introduce complementary foods at **6 months** (strong, low certainty)
- **Rec 4a: "Animal source foods, including meat, fish, or eggs, should be consumed daily" (STRONG, low certainty)** — rationale: when animal-source foods were excluded from diets of 6–8 month olds, needs for **iron, zinc and vitamin B12 could not be met**
- Rec 4b: fruits and vegetables daily (strong); Rec 4c: pulses/nuts/seeds frequently, *particularly when meat/fish/eggs are limited* (conditional, very low)
- Rec 5a: foods high in sugar, salt, trans fats should not be consumed (strong); 5b: no sugar-sweetened beverages (strong); 5c: no non-sugar sweeteners (strong); 5d: limit 100% fruit juice (conditional)
- Rec 6: MNPs, fortified cereals, and SQ-LNS are **context-specific** recommendations (SQ-LNS: high certainty, food-insecure populations)
- Rec 7: responsive feeding (strong, low certainty)

**Canada (CPS + Health Canada + Dietitians of Canada + BCC)**
- "The first complementary foods introduced at about 6 months should be **iron-rich (e.g., meat, meat alternatives, and iron-fortified cereals)**" — https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/iron-requirements
- Health Canada 6–24 month guidance recommends iron-rich foods **two or more times a day, daily** (beef, pork, fish, poultry, tofu, beans, eggs) — Canada.ca page 403s to fetch; restated at https://cps.ca/en/documents/position/iron-requirements and https://thinkbeef.ca/canadian-guidelines-recommend-meats-as-babys-first-solid-food/

**AAP**
- Encourage introduction of iron-rich complementary foods (meat, iron-fortified cereal) from 4–6 months. Whole cow's milk **not before 12 months**. Universal Hb screening + risk assessment at **12 months**. — https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2011/0301/p624.html

**Meat-as-first-food RCT evidence (the strongest single trial)**
- Krebs et al.: 88 exclusively breastfed infants randomized at ~4 months to **pureed beef vs iron-fortified infant cereal** as first complementary food (5→7 months, followed to 12 months). At 7 months, **zinc intake from complementary foods: meat 1.9 ±0.2 mg/day vs cereal 0.6 ±0.1 mg/day** (cereal group ≈25% of EAR). Tolerance/acceptance comparable. Meat group had greater head-circumference increase 7–12 months; zinc and protein intake predicted head growth; trend toward higher behavioral scores at 12 months. **Even so, ~20% had low plasma zinc and 30–40% low ferritin** — i.e. one food is not a complete fix. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16456417/

**Iron-fortified infant cereal — pros and cons**
- PRO: in US data, infant cereal is the **top single source of iron for 6–11.9 month olds, 38.6% of total iron intake** — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5537847/
- CON (bioavailability): infant cereals are commonly fortified with **insoluble, low-bioavailability iron compounds** (ferric pyrophosphate, electrolytic iron) chosen for organoleptic stability. Measured geometric mean bioavailability in infants: **ferrous fumarate 4.1% (range 1.7–14.7%) vs ferric pyrophosphate 1.3% (range 0.7–2.7%)** — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10837304/ / https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(23)07205-2/fulltext . Counterpoint: electrolytic iron and ferrous fumarate performed **equally** as cereal fortificants in breastfed infants — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21178077/ / https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(22)02473-7/fulltext
- CON (arsenic): FDA action level for inorganic arsenic in infant rice cereal = **100 ppb** (final guidance issued Aug 2020, under "Closer to Zero"). 2018 sampling: **76% of samples at or below 100 ppb**, vs 47% in 2014 and 36% in 2011–2013. — https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2020/08/06/2020-17169/ , https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-issues-final-guidance-industry-action-level-inorganic-arsenic-infant-rice-cereals (both 403/404 to automated fetch; figures via https://cen.acs.org/environment/persistent-pollutants/US-FDA-sets-voluntary-arsenic-limit-in-infant-rice-cereal/98/web/2020/08 and Food Safety Magazine)
- Rice **takes up roughly 10× more arsenic from the environment than other grains** — https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/feeding-nutrition/Pages/reduce-arsenic.aspx
- AAP/FDA consumer line: rice cereal **need not be the first cereal**; serve iron-fortified **oat, barley, or multigrain** instead/as well; "there is no evidence to support" rice cereal as the default first food. Other first-food options named: finely chopped meat, vegetable purées. — https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/feeding-nutrition/Pages/reduce-arsenic.aspx , https://publications.aap.org/aapnews/news/9164/
- **Excess-iron caution (relevant to routine fortification in iron-replete infants):** associations with delayed psychomotor development, impaired cognition, poorer growth, increased morbidity, adverse gut microbiota shifts and intestinal inflammation — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8707490/ . So "iron-rich food daily," not "maximize iron."

## 3. HEME vs NON-HEME; ENHANCERS AND INHIBITORS; ZINC

**Absorption rates**
- Heme (animal): commonly cited **up to ~25%**, sometimes quoted 15–35% — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8707490/ , https://cdn.nutrition.org/article/S2475-2991(22)10592-5/fulltext (this last 403s; restated in search index)
- Non-heme (plant/fortificant): far lower and diet-dependent; non-heme makes up **~90% of children's dietary iron** — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8707490/
- Red-meat heme ~**25%** bioavailable vs iron-fortified cereal-based foods ~**10%** (and measured infant-cereal fortificant values of 1.3–4.1%, above)
- Meat also **enhances non-heme iron absorption from co-eaten plant foods** ("meat factor") — the practical implication: adding a small amount of liver/fish/egg to lugaw does double duty.

**Vitamin C — real effect size, with the honest caveat**
- Single-meal isotope studies (Hallberg): **50 mg ascorbic acid ≈ 3× increase; 100 mg ≈ 4× increase** in non-heme iron absorption; "about 50 mg of the vitamin in each main meal is desirable for optimum effect"; mechanism = countering phytates and tannins. n=299 subjects. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3700141/
- **CAVEAT — this is the most commonly over-claimed number.** Cook & Reddy (AJCN 2001), measuring a *complete diet* rather than single meals, found the facilitating effect "far less pronounced"; with a high-ascorbate diet (molar ratio 47.0) between-subject responses ranged from a *decrease* to a 3.5-fold increase. — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11124756/ / https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(23)06453-5/fulltext
- Net: pairing vitamin C with plant iron is real and worth doing, but the "3–4×" figure is a single-meal ceiling, not a daily-diet expectation.

**Inhibitors — with nuance**
- **Phytate** (rice, maize, legumes, unrefined grains): potent; humans lack an endogenous phytase. Hurrell & Egli 2010, AJCN — https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(23)01839-7/fulltext (403 to fetch; widely restated). **Soaking works:** in the Philippine study below, soaking mung beans cut phytate from 275.6 → 151.0 mg/100 g (**45% reduction**), dropping the phytate:zinc molar ratio from 13.8 → 8.1. Fermentation also degrades phytate and tannins.
- **Tea/polyphenols:** black tea with a control meal reduced iron absorption **21%** in one study; across studies 150–300 mL tea gave **3–27%** reductions depending on food; absorption 18.2% (control) vs 7.1% (150 mL tea) and 19.7% vs 5.6% (300 mL tea) in one dataset. Isolated tannic acid: 16–25% reduction. BUT: only 4 of 16 single-meal condensed-tannin studies found reductions, and **most epidemiologic/long-term studies did not show worse iron status with tannin-rich diets**. No infant-specific data in the review. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5998341/
- **Calcium:** classically listed as an inhibitor, but a J Nutr study found **calcium does NOT inhibit absorption of 5 mg non-heme or heme iron at doses <800 mg** in non-pregnant women — https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(22)03044-9/fulltext . The stronger, better-evidenced calcium/milk concern in infancy is cow's milk as a *drink* (see §5), not calcium in a meal.

**Zinc — the co-limiting nutrient**
- Iron and zinc are limited by the *same* foods and the *same* inhibitor (phytate), so a low-iron complementary diet is nearly always a low-zinc diet.
- South/Southeast Asia: estimated prevalence of inadequate intake in infants **6–11.9 months = 75% for iron and 20% for zinc** — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8724761/
- Same paper: the six priority micronutrients most commonly lacking regionally are **iron, zinc, vitamin A, folate, vitamin B12, calcium**. Highest aggregate priority-micronutrient density: **organ meats (liver, kidney, heart, spleen), bivalves (clams/mussels/oysters), crustaceans, fresh fish, goat, canned fish with bones, eggs**; then beef, lamb, dark green leafy vegetables, milk/yogurt/cheese. For **iron specifically**: organs, fresh fish, bivalves, goat rank highest. For **zinc**: organs, bivalves, fresh fish, crustaceans, beef, lamb, eggs.
- Breast milk zinc is sufficient through ~4–6 months only — https://abm.memberclicks.net/assets/DOCUMENTS/PROTOCOLS/29-iron-zinc-and-vitamin-d-protocol-english.pdf
- Krebs RCT quantifies the gap: meat 1.9 vs cereal 0.6 mg zinc/day at 7 months (above).

## 1. Repeated exposure — the numbers, and where "8–15" actually comes from

**The provenance of "8–15" is more nuanced than the popular citation suggests.** Sullivan & Birch (1990, *Developmental Psychology*, "Pass the sugar, pass the salt: experience dictates preference") tested **preschoolers**, not infants — tofu (sweetened/salted/plain) twice weekly for 8 weeks, with preference measured after 1, 8, and 15 exposures. Preference for sweetened/salted tofu emerged by exposure 8; preference for *plain* tofu only emerged after exposure 15. The widely-repeated "8–15 exposures" figure is the 3–4-year-old number, and it gets misapplied to infants.
- https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK610950/
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2716720/

**For infants the number is lower and the evidence is better.** NESR/USDA systematic review (Spill et al., *AJCN* 2019; 21 studies — 19 controlled trials + 2 cohorts; 16 emphasized in synthesis, 12 RCTs):
> "**Moderate evidence** indicates that tasting a single or multiple vegetable(s) or fruit(s), 1 food per day, for **8–10 or more days** is likely to increase acceptability of an exposed food (increased intake or faster rate of feeding) in infants and toddlers **4–24 months**."
- Range across included studies: **6 to 47 total exposures**, over **6 days to 3 months**, mostly 1 exposure/day (range 2×/week to 2×/day).
- Some studies saw change after as few as **1, 3, 5, or 6** exposures.
- Effects **generalize within a food category but not across categories** (veg exposure → other veg, not → fruit).
- Explicit caveat: "some children may never like a particular food regardless of the number of exposures."
- https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK582166/
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0002916522032439

**Maier et al. (2007), 7-month-olds, initially disliked vegetable:** repeated exposure (8 exposures over 16 days) raised intake ~4-fold; by the 8th exposure intake reached **174 ± 54 g**, statistically indistinguishable from the *liked* vegetable. At **9-month follow-up, 63% still liked and ate** the initially-disliked vegetable. *(Numbers sourced from secondary syntheses — the ScienceDirect primary returned HTTP 403, so exact CI/n could not be verified directly.)*
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950329307000523 (403 on fetch)
- Corroborating synthesis: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3363345/

**Other infant exposure dose findings** (from the Frontiers systematic review, Nekitsing/Blundell-Birtill/Hetherington 2017):
- Forestell & Mennella (2007): **8 days** of exposure **tripled** green bean consumption in 4–8-month-olds.
- Sullivan & Birch (1994): **10** taste exposures increased intake in 4–6-month-olds.
- Birch et al. (1998): a **single** exposure increased intake in 4–7-month-olds.
- Hausner et al. (2012): intake changed by the **5th** presentation under plain repeated exposure vs. the **10th** under flavor-flavor learning.
- Caton et al. (2014): children **>24 months showed less change** in acceptance following exposure than younger infants — i.e., the intervention gets harder with age.
- https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01046/full
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5524770/

**The give-up gap.** Carruth, Ziegler, Gordon & Barr (2004), large survey of caregivers of infants/toddlers 4–24 months (FITS data): caregivers offered a new food only **3–5 times** before concluding the child disliked it. This is the canonical source for "parents quit too early," and it is roughly half to a third of the evidence-based dose.
- https://www.jpedhc.org/article/S0891-5245(05)00117-3/fulltext
- https://www.researchgate.net/publication/8937338

**Cochrane (Hodder et al., latest update 53 trials / 120 arms / 12,350 participants):** **moderate-certainty** evidence that child-feeding-practice interventions (repeated exposure, rewards) are probably effective at increasing vegetable intake in under-5s — but effects are demonstrated mainly **short-term (<12 months)**. 16 trials specifically on child-feeding practices.
- https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD008552.pub8/full

## 2. Variety and timing — the strongest single lever in the literature

**Gerrish & Mennella (2001), "Flavor variety enhances food acceptance in formula-fed infants," *AJCN*.** ~6-month-olds accepted a novel food (carrot) better if previously exposed to **carrot itself** or to **a variety of foods changing day to day** — but *not* if repeatedly exposed to a single different food (potato). Variety produces **generalization** to genuinely novel foods; single-food exposure does not.
- https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11955661_Flavor_variety_enhance_food_acceptance_in_formula-fed_infants
- https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01046/full

**Maier et al. (2008), *Clinical Nutrition*, "Breastfeeding and experience with variety early in weaning increase infants' acceptance of new foods for up to two months."** Design: first vegetable = carrot purée, then over 9 days either (a) carrot daily, (b) 3 vegetables changed **every 3 days**, or (c) 3 vegetables changed **daily**. Key result: **frequency of change was more effective than the number of vegetables fed.** Breastfeeding + high variety produced greatest new-food intake; effect persisted **2 months later**. Directly contradicts the folk rule of "introduce one new food at a time and wait."
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18838198/

**Maier-Nöth et al. (2016), *PLOS ONE* — the long-run follow-up, and the best evidence that early variety persists.** n=147 dyads (Aalen DE, Dijon FR), mean age 5.2 months at entry; retention 107 (73%) at 15 mo, 96 (65%) at 3 y, 75 (51%) at 6 y. Arms: no variety (carrot ×10 d) / low variety (3 veg, 3 consecutive days each) / high variety (3 veg changed daily).

At **age 6 years**, high-variety group vs. low/no:
- New-vegetable **liking**: 4.4±0.3 vs. 2.5±0.3 and 2.9±0.3, **p=0.0002**
- New-vegetable **intake**: 14.1±1.5 g vs. 4.3±1.5 and 3.2±1.4 g, **p<0.0001**
- **Willingness to taste**: 8.9±0.6 vegetables vs. 5.6±0.6 each, **p=0.0001**
- *Familiar* vegetable intake: 19.6±2.0 g vs. 13.1±2.0 and 13.1±1.9 g, **p=0.03**
- Breastfed children ate more new veg (9.0±1.0 g vs. 5.4±1.4 g, p=0.04) and tasted more (7.8±0.4 vs. 5.6±0.6, p=0.004)
- Initially-disliked vegetable: still positively rated by **57%** at age 6 (79% at 15 mo, 73% at 3 y)

**Critically for parental expectations:** the variety effect was **NOT detectable at 15 months (p=0.20) or 3 years (p=0.0635)** — it re-emerged at 6. Authors attribute the gap to the normal 2–3-year-old tendency to refuse both novel *and* previously accepted foods. This is the empirical answer to "my early wins wore off."
- https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0151356
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26968029/

**Hetherington et al. (2015), *Br J Nutr* 114(2):328–36 (HabEat exploratory trial, UK/Greece/Portugal).** Infants given **a variety of single vegetables as first foods during the first 15 days** of complementary feeding ate **twice as much** of a novel vegetable (artichoke purée) one month post-intervention vs. infants starting with fruit + veg + infant rice.
- https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/an-exploratory-trial-of-parental-advice-for-increasing-vegetable-acceptance-in-infancy/346443558B01D18C1C6CFD5B566CC474
- Open PDF: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/118790/3/TASTE%20paper%20BJN%20submission%20(1).pdf

### The "flavor window" — weaker than the marketing claims

**The popular "6–18 month flavor window" is not what the sensitive-period RCT actually found.** Mennella et al. (2011, *AJCN*, "The timing and duration of a sensitive period in human flavor learning: a randomized trial"): 47 formula-feeding mothers (2006–2009), infants randomized at 2 weeks to cow-milk formula for 8 mo (n=13) or extensively hydrolyzed protein formula (bitter/sour/savory) for **1 mo (n=11), 3 mo (n=11), or 8 mo (n=11)**. At 8.5 months, tested on savory (MSG) broth. Infants exposed for **3 or 8 months** consumed significantly more savory broth and ate faster (p<0.01); **1 month of exposure did nothing.**
- Authors' conclusion: a sensitive period **<4 months of age**; the window "is beginning to close at ≈3.5 mo."
- Authors' own caveats, verbatim in spirit: "one cannot assume that there is only one sensitive period in flavor programming," and "windows of plasticity in flavor learning may not shut abruptly."
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434272/
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21310829/

**Assessment: the "flavor window" as marketed by baby-food brands (6–18 months) is a commercial reframing.** The one RCT designed to test a sensitive period found a *much earlier and narrower* one, for formula flavor specifically. The defensible evidence-based claim is a **gradient of declining plasticity**, not a door that shuts — supported by Caton 2014 (>24 months = less responsive) and Maier-Nöth 2016 (effects of 5–6-month variety still detectable at 6 years). Commercial framings: https://myserenitykids.com/blogs/news/introducing-the-flavor-window, https://www.mother.ly/baby/baby-milestones/flavor-window-starting-solids/

## 3. Flavor learning before solids (amniotic fluid + breast milk)

**Mennella, Jagnow & Beauchamp (2001), *Pediatrics* 107(6):E88 — the carrot-juice RCT.** Three groups: (a) carrot juice in last 3 weeks of pregnancy + water during first 2 months lactation; (b) water in pregnancy + carrot juice during lactation; (c) water in both. At weaning, infants exposed to carrot in **either** amniotic fluid or breast milk showed **fewer negative facial expressions** eating carrot-flavored cereal vs. plain cereal; controls showed no such difference. Prenatally-exposed infants were rated by mothers as enjoying the carrot cereal more.
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11389286/
- https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1351272/

**NESR/Spahn et al. (2019), *AJCN* systematic review — the authoritative grading.** Search of 10 databases, Jan 1980–Jun 2017; 11 articles on pregnancy, 15 on lactation.
- **Pregnancy/amniotic fluid: "LIMITED but consistent evidence"** that flavors (alcohol, anise, carrot, garlic) transfer to and flavor amniotic fluid, and that fetal exposure increases acceptance of similarly-flavored foods on re-exposure during infancy and *potentially* childhood.
- **Lactation/breast milk: "MODERATE evidence"** that flavors (alcohol, anise/caraway, carrot, eucalyptus, garlic, mint) transmit to breast milk **in a time-dependent manner**.
- Note the asymmetry: the evidence that flavors *transfer* is stronger than the evidence that transfer *changes long-term acceptance*.
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30982867/
- https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK582184/
- https://nesr.usda.gov/sites/default/files/2022-11/Maternal-diet-AF-and-BM-flavor-NESR-Systematic-Review.pdf

**Breastfeeding as an amplifier:** Maier 2008 found breastfeeding **enhanced the effect of variety** on later acceptance of new foods; Maier-Nöth 2016 found breastfed children still tasted more and ate more new vegetables at age 6 (p=0.004 / p=0.04). Mechanism proposed: breast milk is itself a variety exposure (flavor varies with maternal diet); formula is flavor-constant.

## 4. Textures — the strongest "hard deadline" in the whole literature

**Northstone et al. (2001), *J Hum Nutr Diet*, ALSPAC, n=9,360.** Distribution of lumpy-solid introduction: **10.7% before 6 mo, 71.7% at 6–9 mo, 17.6% at ≥10 mo.**
- Earliest group ate a greater variety of family foods at 6 months.
- **≥10 months group:** significantly less likely to be eating family foods at 15 months; "more difficult to feed"; "more definite likes and dislikes."
- Feeding difficulties **32% vs. 52%** comparing early vs. late introduction.
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11301932/
- https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1046/j.1365-277x.2001.00264.x

**Coulthard, Harris & Emmett (2009), *Matern Child Nutr*, ALSPAC follow-up, n=7,821 — the age-7 outcome.** Groups: **12.1% <6 mo, 69.8% 6–9 mo, 18.1% >9 mo.** Children introduced to lumpy foods **after 9 months** had, at **age 7**: more eating difficulties, and ate **fewer fruits and vegetables** and fewer key food groups.
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19161546/

**Mechanistic corroboration — Demonteil/Nicklaus, longitudinal 6–18 months:** chewing **emerges at 8 months, well established at 10 months.** Texture acceptance ladder: sticky textures from **8 mo**, hard foods from **12 mo**, raw pieces from **18 mo**. Acceptance of hard textures coincides with emergence of chewing.
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950329318304051
- https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2020.616484/full

**Guidance convergence:** WHO recommends lumpy foods between **6 and 9 months**. ESPGHAN Committee on Nutrition members advise **avoiding prolonged puree use** and getting infants onto lumpy foods **by 8–10 months at the latest.**
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950329318304051
- NHS: "It may take **10 tries or more** for your baby to get used to new foods, flavours and textures" — https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/weaning-and-feeding/babys-first-solid-foods/

**Caveat: this is observational (ALSPAC cohort), not randomized.** Reverse causation is plausible — infants who were already difficult feeders may have had lumps delayed *because* of it. No RCT has randomized texture timing to a hard age cutoff. A pilot texture-introduction intervention (8–15 months) exists but is small: https://sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0195666320316111

## 5. Vegetables and bitter tastes — veg-first has real but modest measured effects

**Barends et al. (2019), *Appetite*, "A systematic review of practices to promote vegetable acceptance in the first three years of life."** 46 papers: 25 experimental/intervention + 21 observational (Scopus + PubMed, pre-March 2018). Conclusions:
- Repeated exposure increases acceptance of **the target vegetable**;
- **Exposure to variety is "particularly effective" for increasing acceptance of a NEW vegetable** (generalization);
- Three most promising strategies: **(1) introduce vegetables at the beginning of complementary feeding, (2) give a different type of vegetable every day, (3) repeat exposure to the same vegetable after an interval of a few days.**
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0195666318307955
- https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/143246/

**Barends et al. (2013/2014) — the honest mixed result.** Groups received vegetable purées every other day for **18 consecutive days**; green beans as target for one group, artichoke for the other. Mean vegetable intake rose from **24 ± 28 g (days 1–2) to 45 ± 44 g (days 17–18)** — significant. **But: green beans and plums increased significantly, while artichoke intake stayed low and apple rose only slightly.** Repeated exposure does not rescue every vegetable.
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S095032931300044X
- https://research.wur.nl/en/publications/effects-of-repeated-exposure-to-either-vegetables-or-fruits-on-in

**The NZ "vegetables first" RCT (Wall et al., 2022, *AJCN* 116(1):111).** Randomized parallel-group, **n=117** Auckland infants: **vegetables only (n=61)** vs. **fruit + vegetables (n=56)** for **4 weeks** from day 1 of complementary feeding (~4–6 mo). Primary outcome at **9 months**: intake of study-provided broccoli and spinach.
- Broccoli: **median 47 g vs. 30 g**; spinach: **37 g vs. 24 g** — roughly **double**.
- Home daily vegetable consumption **28% higher** in veg-only group.
- Veg-only infants ate target vegetables **faster** and accepted at a greater rate.
- **Null on liking:** maternal liking ratings did **not** differ by group.
- Notable: in controls, **pear was eaten in significantly greater amounts than broccoli/spinach**, whereas the veg-only group "ate just as much vegetables as fruit."
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35679432/
- https://academic.oup.com/ajcn/article/116/1/111/6604981
- Open copy: https://mro.massey.ac.nz/bitstreams/8ce75529-aaed-46fa-bcec-4db844e9a655/download

**Baby's First Bites RCT (Netherlands) — the important negative result.** 4-arm RCT, **n=246** first-time Dutch mothers: Repeated Vegetable Exposure (RVE) / video-feedback sensitive-feeding (VIPP-FI) / Combined (COMBI) / attention control. Five sessions, child age 4–16 months; outcomes at 18 and 24 months.
- **Vegetable intake at t18: RVE 90 g, VIPP-FI 95 g, COMBI 85 g, AC 79 g/day — NOT significant (p=0.11–0.86).** At t24: 75 / 84 / 80 / 70 g — also NS.
- **No intervention effect on self-regulation of energy intake** (eating-in-absence-of-hunger, p=0.91).
- **No BMI-z differences** on continuous measures (p=0.29–0.82); only overweight-prevalence differences (COMBI 2% vs. VIPP-FI 16% at t18, p=0.02; COMBI 7% vs. AC 20% at t24, p=0.02) — authors say interpret cautiously, small numbers.
- **What DID work:** VIPP-FI/COMBI improved *maternal* responsiveness to satiety cues at 18 mo (d=0.55, d=0.47) and reduced pressure to eat (d=0.35–0.47) — **but all effects disappeared by 24 months.**
- Authors' explanation for the nulls: ceiling effect — participants had "above average interest in infant food products and healthy eating practices," giving a well-performing control group.
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8826930/
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31370830/

**Net read on veg-first:** two RCTs (NZ, HabEat/UK) show ~2× intake of target vegetables at short-to-medium follow-up; one large 4-arm RCT (Baby's First Bites) shows **null on the primary child outcomes**. It's cheap and carries no downside, but it's not a guarantee, and the measured effect is on *intake*, not always on *liking*.

## 6. Feeding style — pressure, responsiveness, Satter, modeling, neophobia

### Pressure to eat backfires (the best-replicated finding in this section)

- **Generation R (longitudinal, population-based):** preschool fussy eating at 4 y independently associated with parental pressure to eat; **pressure at 4 y independently associated with MORE fussiness at 6 y.** Bidirectional, but the parent→child path is real and "if anything, counterproductive."
  - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031938416311015
- **Recollections of pressure predict adult eating:** childhood **pressure** to eat — but **not** childhood picky eating — predicted intuitive-eating deficits and disordered-eating symptoms in college students.
  - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0195666315300970
- **AHA Scientific Statement, "Caregiver Influences on Eating Behaviors in Young Children":** pressure is "associated with an impaired ability to self-regulate eating behaviors in preschool"; **restriction** is associated with "higher consumption of food when not hungry." Recommends **structure over control** (consistent meal/snack routines, timing, selective availability) and an **authoritative** rather than authoritarian feeding style.
  - Also endorses: repeatedly offering foods + caregivers **"enthusiastically consuming the food themselves"**; peer modeling.
  - **Honest self-assessment in the statement:** there is "relatively little empirical evidence" demonstrating benefits of responsive, structured approaches — most of the evidence is about the *harms of less responsive feeding*, not the *benefits of intervention*.
  - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7660848/

### Satter's Division of Responsibility — popular, plausible, thinly evidenced as an intervention

- **What it is:** parent decides *what, when, where*; child decides *whether* and *how much*.
  - https://www.ellynsatterinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/ELLYN-SATTER%E2%80%99S-DIVISION-OF-RESPONSIBILITY-IN-FEEDING.pdf
- **Evidence base is mostly measurement + cross-sectional association, not RCTs.** A validated adherence scale exists (**sDOR.2-6y**, ages 2–6): each **1-point increase decreased nutrition-risk odds by 21%** in low-income mothers. Construct validity established observationally.
  - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1499404620307168
  - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212267220314830
- **The critique, sourced:** reviews of responsive feeding note "**lack of effectiveness trials**," that "one-half of the studies were observational with cross-sectional designs," that "few interventions were designed to isolate the effect of responsive feeding," and that "cross-study comparisons were difficult due to multiple definitions of responsive feeding."
  - https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(22)02504-4/fulltext
  - https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(22)02948-0/fulltext
- **Fair summary:** DOR is *consistent with* the strong pressure-backfires evidence and with authoritative-feeding associations, and is endorsed by WHO's responsive-feeding recommendation — but **rated Strong recommendation / LOW certainty evidence** by WHO itself. It is a well-reasoned framework built on solid negative evidence (don't pressure), not a proven positive intervention.
  - https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK596430/

### Responsive-parenting RCT evidence (INSIGHT)

INSIGHT (responsive parenting, randomized): control mothers used more pressure when child satiety responsiveness was high; **RP-group mothers used consistently lower pressure regardless** — i.e., the intervention "protected against the typical maternal response of pressure." Also reduced **use of food to soothe** and increased alternative soothing. At 9 months, RP infants had higher odds of a "formula, fruits and vegetables" dietary pattern.
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7812701/
- https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12966-018-0700-6
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12085782/

### Modeling and family meals

- **Meta-analysis (Yee et al., *IJBNPA* 2017):** parental **modeling** had one of the strongest associations with healthy food consumption, **r = .32, p < .001**; **availability** r = .24, p < .001. Small-to-moderate effect sizes.
  - https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12966-017-0501-3
  - https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28399881/
- **Family-meal meta-analysis:** parental modeling of healthy eating positively associated with children's nutritional health (**r = .12**), alongside TV-off during meals and longer meal duration.
  - https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_3172431/component/file_3177504/content
- **Prospective review of parental feeding practices (1–6 y):** **structure-based** practices (modeling, availability, routines) were most frequently *positively* associated with fruit/veg consumption.
  - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0195666325001655

### Neophobia: a normal developmental phase, and substantially genetic

- **Peak:** highest severity **between ages 2 and 6**; rises in early childhood, declines through adolescence/adulthood, may re-emerge in later life.
  - https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11396904/
- **Heritability is high:** twin studies give **h² ≈ 0.72–0.78** for food neophobia; MZ vs DZ correlations r = 0.71 vs. r = -0.01 in one study; ~22% of variance from **non-shared** environment, with **no detectable shared-environment effect** in some models. Practical implication: a meaningful share of the outcome is not under parental control, and the parenting-controllable variance runs through *non-shared* (child-specific) experience.
  - https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(23)13315-6/fulltext
  - https://www.researchgate.net/publication/47450927

### Picky eating: prevalence, trajectory, persistence (Taylor & Emmett, *Proc Nutr Soc* 2019, ALSPAC)

- **Prevalence by age (ALSPAC):** 24 mo **10%** → 38 mo **15% (peak)** → 54 mo **14%** → 65 mo **12%**. Peak ≈ age 3.
- **Four trajectories** identified: persistent, remitting, late-onset, never picky. Early-onset picky eating is more stable. Norwegian cohort: **50% picky at both 4 and 6 years.**
- **Risk factors:** first-year feeding difficulties; **late introduction of lumpy foods (after 9 months)**; **vegetables introduced after 6 months vs. 4–5 months**; ready-prepared foods; maternal smoking in pregnancy; child choosiness at 15 mo strongly predicts picky eating at 38 mo. **Maternal worry about the choosiness raises risk from 17% to 50%** — the single most striking number in the review.
- **Protective:** fresh foods; **shared family meals** where everyone eats the same food.
- **Consequences in persistent picky eaters:** **40% less** carcass meat, **33% less** fruit; zinc intakes **3–16% lower**; reduced iron; markedly lower fiber (constipation risk). **Energy intake generally adequate**; weights/heights "consistently above the 50th centile" though lower than non-picky peers. A small subgroup shows increased thinness risk in adolescence.
- **On exposures:** the review states "**10–15 positive experiences** may be needed" before children accept unfamiliar foods.
  - https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-nutrition-society/article/picky-eating-in-children-causes-and-consequences/34921F967B9F37046962CA866DC199C3

## 7. Baby-led weaning vs. spoon-feeding — the honest verdict

**BLISS RCT (Taylor et al., Dunedin NZ). n=206** women randomized to control (usual care) or BLISS (modified BLW — 8 contacts antenatal→9 mo, plus explicit choking-risk modifications: iron-rich food at each meal, high-energy food at each meal, and avoidance of choking-hazard food shapes). Follow-up to 24 months.
- https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12887-015-0491-8
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28692728/

**Choking (Fangupo et al., 2016, *Pediatrics* 138(4):e20160772):**
- **35% of infants choked at least once between 6 and 8 months** — in *both* groups.
- **No significant group differences in number of choking events at any time point.**
- Gagging: BLISS group **higher at 6 months, LOWER at 8 months** than controls.
- **Crucial caveats stated by the authors:** "high proportions of infants **in both groups** were offered foods posing a choking risk," "infants were **not consistently closely supervised** while eating," and "a **small number of serious choking events** were observed in both study groups."
- **Interpretation discipline:** this trial tested *BLISS* — BLW **with structured professional safety coaching** — not unguided BLW. It shows BLW-with-support isn't riskier; it does not license unsupervised BLW.
  - https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article-abstract/138/4/e20160772/52372/
  - https://www.healio.com/news/pediatrics/20161005/choking-hazards-similar-for-bliss-traditional-feeding-methods-among-infants

**Fussiness / enjoyment / self-regulation:**
- **12 months:** less food fussiness (adjusted difference **−0.33; 95% CI −0.51 to −0.14**) and greater enjoyment of food (**+0.25; 95% CI 0.07 to 0.43**) in BLISS.
- **24 months:** the **fussiness advantage did NOT persist**; enjoyment of food remained higher.
- **Against BLW's central claim:** BLISS infants showed **LOWER satiety responsiveness at 24 months** (adjusted difference **−0.24; 95% CI −0.41 to −0.07**) — the opposite direction to the self-regulation argument that motivates BLW.
- **BMI:** no significant difference at 24 months (control 0.24 [1.01] vs. BLISS 0.39 [1.04]; adjusted difference **0.16; 95% CI −0.13 to 0.45**).
  - https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28692728/
  - https://www.healio.com/news/pediatrics/20170714/baby-led-weaning-results-in-less-food-fussiness-with-no-difference-in-bmi

**Observational literature runs much hotter than the RCT** — and should be discounted accordingly. A 2024–25 cross-sectional study in **Metro Manila, Philippines** (n=284 parents, children 2–5 y, CEBQ) reported strict BLW associated with **95.4% lower odds of picky eating (aOR = 0.05)**, with a dose-response by autonomy. Authors' own limitations: cross-sectional (reverse causation possible), self-report, selection bias toward higher-educated families, unmeasured SES/parenting-style confounders, single instrument. **An aOR of 0.05 from a cross-sectional convenience sample is almost certainly confounded** — but it is directly relevant given the Philippines context.
  - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12964498/
- NZ internet survey also reported lower food fussiness with BLW: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0195666318305580

**Verdict:** BLW's demonstrated RCT advantages are **modest and partly transient** — less fussiness and more enjoyment at 12 months, enjoyment persisting to 24 months, fussiness advantage gone by 24 months, **no BMI benefit**, and a **satiety-responsiveness result pointing the wrong way**. Choking risk is not elevated *when done with the BLISS safety modifications*. It is a reasonable choice, not a decisive one; the variety/texture/exposure levers have stronger evidence.

## 8. Sweet and salt

### Sweet

- **Innate and developmentally amplified, not learned.** Children are born preferring sweet; **preference is heightened above adult levels throughout childhood and early adolescence, declining to adult levels in mid-to-late adolescence — coinciding with cessation of physical growth.** Mennella's work links sweet/salt preference to growth markers (Ntx bone-growth marker, sweet-receptor genotype). Implication: a toddler preferring sweet is **normal biology**, not a parenting failure.
  - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0002916523049997
  - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S096098221300208X
  - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1499404620307168 *(context)*
  - https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/03/140317174307.htm
- **Does early sugar exposure raise later sweet preference? Evidence is medium-specific and shakier than usually claimed.** Beauchamp & Moran: infants fed sweetened water in the first 6 months maintained a preference for sweetened water at 2 years — **but the association did NOT replicate when sweetness liking was tested with a fruit-flavoured drink instead of sweetened water.** The learning appears **stimulus-specific** (sweetened water → sweetened water), not a global "sweet tooth."
  - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10024975/
  - https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/sweet-tooth-of-infancy-is-sweetness-exposure-related-to-sweetness-liking-in-infants-up-to-12-months-of-age/A90269F51A0CBAF676D094A3501C7099
- **Counter-evidence worth noting:** follow-up of a Ghanaian RCT found early exposure to a slightly sweet lipid-based nutrient supplement did **NOT** increase preference for or consumption of sweet foods/beverages at ages 4–6.
  - https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6398382/
  - https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6462430/

**AHA "no added sugar before 2" — what it's actually based on.** Vos et al., AHA Scientific Statement, *Circulation*, August 2016:
> "It is reasonable to recommend that children consume **≤25 g (100 cal, ≈6 teaspoons)** of added sugars per day and to **avoid added sugars for children <2 years of age**."
- **The evidence cited is cardiometabolic, not taste-preference:** "Strong evidence supports the association of added sugars with increased cardiovascular disease risk in children through increased energy intake, increased adiposity, and dyslipidemia," and associations appear "at levels far below current consumption levels."
- **The under-2 rationale is specifically a nutrient-density/displacement argument:** calorie needs in this age group are low, so "there is little room for food and beverages containing added sugars that don't provide good nutrition." It is **not** primarily an argument that sugar before 2 permanently programs a sweet tooth.
  - https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/cir.0000000000000439
  - https://publications.aap.org/aapnews/news/7400/AHA-Limit-children-s-sugar-consumption-to-6
  - https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/08/160822174100.htm
- **WHO 2023 concurs at Strong recommendation / Low certainty:** avoid foods high in sugar, salt, trans fats; "sugar-sweetened beverages should not be consumed"; avoid non-sugar sweeteners; limit 100% fruit juice (conditional).
  - https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK596430/

### Salt

- **Salt preference is substantially LEARNED, in contrast to sweet.** Stein, Cowart & Beauchamp prospective study, **61 infants tested at 2 and 6 months**: newborns are indifferent/averse to salt; acceptance emerges around 4 months and **tracks dietary exposure**. Increased acceptance of sodium-rich foods "coincides with infants' exposure to salty foods, and is therefore thought to be mostly a learned response."
  - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0002916523026084
  - https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/12/111220154045.htm
  - https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5622771/
- **The natural experiment:** **169 adolescents** exposed during infancy to a **chloride-deficient formula** showed increased liking for salty foods vs. their closest-aged siblings — a long-range effect of early salt experience.
  - https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8879420/
- **Guidance:** ESPGHAN — "**no sugar or salt should be added** to complementary foods; fruit juices or sugar-sweetened beverages should be avoided." NHS — "do not add sugar or salt (**including stock cubes and gravy**) to your baby's food or cooking water"; **6–12 months: max 1 g salt/day (0.4 g sodium)**; no honey before 12 months.
  - https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1097/MPG.0000000000001454
  - https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28027215/
  - https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/baby/weaning-and-feeding/babys-first-solid-foods/
  - https://www.nhs.uk/baby/weaning-and-feeding/foods-to-avoid-giving-babies-and-young-children/

**Asymmetry worth flagging to the parent:** the salt evidence for early-exposure programming is **stronger** than the sugar evidence. Salt liking is genuinely learned from exposure; sweet liking is largely innate, age-graded, and only weakly/stimulus-specifically shaped by early exposure. The case for avoiding added sugar before 2 rests mainly on displacement and cardiometabolic risk, not on preference programming.

## Cross-cutting notes for the synthesizer

1. **Ranked by evidence strength:** (a) repeated exposure ≥8–10 days — moderate-grade systematic-review evidence, multiple RCTs; (b) daily-changing variety in the first ~2 weeks of complementary feeding — RCT evidence with a 6-year follow-up, the only intervention with demonstrated *long-run* persistence; (c) lumpy textures by 8–10 months — large cohort evidence (ALSPAC ×2), mechanistically corroborated, but observational; (d) don't pressure — strong longitudinal + bidirectional evidence; (e) veg-first — 2 positive RCTs, 1 large null RCT; (f) BLW — modest, partly transient RCT effects; (g) prenatal/lactation flavor exposure — limited-to-moderate graded evidence, real but small.

2. **The 2–3-year "disappearance" is the single most important expectation-setting fact.** Maier-Nöth 2016 found the early-variety effect undetectable at 15 months (p=0.20) and 3 years (p=0.0635) and clearly present again at 6 years (p<0.0001 for intake). Combined with neophobia peaking 2–6 and h²≈0.72–0.78, the honest framing is: early work is *banked*, not *visible*, during the neophobic phase.

3. **The "8–15 exposures" number is misattributed in the popular literature.** It comes from Sullivan & Birch's *preschooler* tofu study. For infants at complementary feeding the defensible figure is **8–10+ daily exposures** (NESR moderate grade), and effects generalize **within** food category only.

4. **Could not verify directly:** the exact n and CIs of the Maier et al. 2007 primary paper (ScienceDirect returned 403; the 174 g and 63%-at-9-months figures come from secondary syntheses including PMC3363345). Also could not verify a primary source for the specific "OTIS trial" (Nordic) *results* — it appears only as a referenced companion trial in the NZ vegetables-first protocol; the substantive veg-first RCT results reported above are from Wall et al. 2022 (NZ) and Hetherington et al. 2015 (HabEat), not OTIS.
