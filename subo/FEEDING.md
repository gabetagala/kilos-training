> Compiled 2026-08-05 from primary trials and current guidelines (NIAID 2017,
> AAP 2019, AAAAI/ACAAI/CSACI 2021, EAACI 2021, ESPGHAN 2017, BSACI, **ASCIA
> 2026**, WHO 2023) plus the developmental-feeding literature. Full raw findings

## 0. The one belief to overturn first

**"Avoid allergens first, then slowly introduce them" is the advice that was
withdrawn.** It was AAP policy in 2000 (delay cow's milk to 1, egg to 2,
peanut/nuts/fish to 3) — issued, by the AAP's own admission at the time,
without conclusive studies behind it. The AAP partially reversed in 2008 and
fully in 2019.

**No current guideline from any major allergy body recommends delaying any
allergen.** Not NIAID, AAP, AAAAI/ACAAI/CSACI, EAACI, ESPGHAN, ASCIA, or
BSACI. The UK's BSACI puts it bluntly: deliberate exclusion or delayed
introduction *may increase* the risk of allergy to those same foods.

**Why delay backfires — the dual-allergen exposure hypothesis (Lack, 2008).**
Sensitisation happens through *skin*; tolerance happens through *gut*. Food
protein is already in the house — on hands, tabletops, dust, our own fingers —
and low-dose contact through an imperfect skin barrier teaches the immune
system to make IgE. Eating the food in real amounts teaches tolerance instead.
So "avoiding" a food orally isn't zero exposure; it's **skin-only exposure** —
all of the sensitising route, none of the protective one. This is also why
eczema matters so much: a broken barrier holds that sensitising door wide open.

Two practical corollaries now written into ASCIA 2026: **don't smear food on
skin to "test" for allergy** (skin contact without eating may itself sensitise),
and keep eczema well controlled while introducing foods.

## 1. The allergen evidence, in numbers

| Trial | Population | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LEAP** (NEJM 2015) | 640 infants, 4–11 mo, **severe eczema and/or egg allergy** | ~6 g peanut protein/wk over ≥3 meals vs avoidance, to age 5 | Peanut allergy at 5 y: **13.7% avoiding vs 1.9% eating** (skin-test-negative cohort) — **86% relative reduction, NNT ~8.5**. Already-sensitised cohort: 35.3% vs 10.6% |
| **LEAP-On** (NEJM 2016) | 550 of the above | All **stopped** peanut for 12 months, age 5→6 | **18.6% vs 4.8%** — tolerance held. It was real tolerance, not suppression |
| **PETIT** (Lancet 2017) | 147 Japanese infants **with eczema** | Stepwise heated egg powder from 6 mo, eczema aggressively treated in *both* arms | Egg allergy at 12 mo: **38% placebo vs 8% egg**. Stopped early for benefit |
| **EAT** (NEJM 2016) | 1303 general-population infants | 6 allergens from 3 mo, 2 g protein 2×/wk | Intention-to-treat **null** — but only ~42% hit the dose. Per-protocol: any food allergy **7.3% → 2.4%**; peanut **2.5% → 0%**; egg **5.5% → 1.4%** |
| **Scarpone meta-analysis** (JAMA Peds 2023) | 4811 (egg) / 3796 (peanut) | Pooled RCTs | Egg **RR 0.60**, peanut **RR 0.31** — both **GRADE high certainty** |

Two honest notes. EAT's headline "null" is an adherence artifact — the babies
who actually ate the target dose were markedly protected, a dose-response
rather than an absence of effect. And the same meta-analysis found **withdrawal
from trials RR 2.29**: pushing large allergen doses into very young infants is
genuinely hard in practice, which is an argument for a relaxed, food-shaped
approach over gram-counting.

**Form is not a detail.** Trials using *raw pasteurised* egg powder (STAR, HEAP)
had to be halted — a third of participants reacted in STAR, including
anaphylaxis. Every guideline now specifies **well-cooked egg only**.

**What it's worth in absolute terms (BSACI's framing).** In the general
population, earlier egg prevents allergy in ~3% of infants and earlier peanut
~2%. For infants with moderate-to-severe eczema: **egg ~19%, peanut ~11%.** And
real-world US data since the guideline change shows infant peanut allergy
falling 0.79% → 0.45%.

## 2. The protocol we'd actually follow

**Start solids around 6 months, not before 4** — when he's developmentally
ready: sits with support, steady head, swallows rather than pushes food out,
coordinates hand to mouth, interested in what we're eating.

**Peanut and egg should not be the very first foods.** Not for safety of the
allergen — for signal clarity. A baby new to solids gags, coughs, and vomits on
*texture*, and that gets misread as an allergic reaction, which then triggers
years of unnecessary avoidance. Get a few plain foods in first so we know what
normal looks like for him, and those same purées become the vehicle for mixing
in peanut.

**Then introduce the allergens early and keep them in.** ASCIA 2026: egg and
peanut "soon after starting solids," irrespective of family history; the other
seven (cow's milk, wheat, soy, sesame, tree nuts, fish, shellfish) within the
first year, prioritising **what our family actually eats**.

**One new allergen per meal — but no mandatory waiting period.** The
"one-at-a-time, wait three days" rule is, in AAAAI's own words, "a more common
sense than evidence-based approach." There's no required gap and no required
order. What matters is one *new* allergen at a time so that if something
happens, we know what caused it. For the same reason: **skip the multi-allergen
commercial powders** — ASCIA explicitly recommends against them, because they
destroy attribution.

**First exposure, at home (NIAID's protocol):**
1. Only when he's well — no cold, vomiting, or diarrhoea.
2. At home. Not daycare, not a restaurant.
3. One adult free of distractions.
4. Set aside **2 hours** afterward to watch him.
5. Small amount **on the tip of a spoon → wait 10 minutes →** if nothing, feed
   the rest at his normal pace.

**Keep each allergen in rotation at least once a week, indefinitely.** This is
the part people miss: introducing a food and then dropping it may itself cause
allergy to develop. ASCIA 2026 makes weekly the explicit standard. LEAP-On's
safe 12-month break came *after five years* of consistent eating — it is not
permission to stop at eight months old. Practically (AAAAI): **1–2 teaspoons of
peanut butter or egg at least once weekly**, more if he likes it. AAAAI is also
refreshingly honest that there's **no known dose-response threshold** — "regular
exposure for several years is felt to be more important than focusing on a
particular fixed dosing interval or amount." The app should track *last served*
per allergen, not grams.

This weekly-rotation nudge is exactly what the ₱999/month subscription is
selling, and it's a `Date` subtraction.

### Amounts, when we want a number

**2 g peanut protein** = 2 level tsp smooth peanut butter (9–10 g) · or 2 level
tsp peanut flour · or ~17 g / 21 pieces of Bamba-style peanut puffs. LEAP's
target was ~6–7 g protein per week across 3+ meals.

### Safe forms — non-negotiable, these are choking rules

- **No whole nuts before age 4** (some sources say 5). Not whole, not in pieces.
- **Never peanut butter off a spoon or in a lump** before 4 — it's a genuine
  airway risk. **Thin it:** 2 tsp peanut butter + 2–3 tsp hot water, stirred
  and cooled, or stirred into 2–3 Tbsp of purée.
- **Egg: well-cooked only** — boiled, scrambled, or baked into pancakes or
  muffins. Never raw or raw-pasteurised.
- **Tree nuts** → nut butters, thinned. Cashew and walnut fit our cooking.
- **Sesame** → tahini or hummus thinned into purée, never sesame candy.
- **Soy** → tofu (we have taho and tokwa on tap). **Not soy sauce** — almost no
  protein, enormous salt load.
- **Fish** → deboned and flaked; oily fish no more than twice a week.
- **Cow's milk** → yogurt and cheese from ~6–7 months, milk baked into food.
  But **not liquid cow's milk as a main drink before 12 months** — that limit
  is about renal solute load and iron, not allergy.
- Also under 12 months: **no honey** (botulism risk).

### Telling a real reaction from a false alarm

**Mild:** a new rash, or a few hives around the mouth or face.

**Get medical help immediately** for any of: lip, face, or tongue swelling ·
widespread hives · vomiting · difficulty breathing, wheeze, or repetitive
cough · colour change (pale or blue) · sudden lethargy or floppiness. In
infants, **behavioural change — inconsolable crying, clinging, unusual
lethargy — counts as a symptom.** IgE reactions usually appear within 30
minutes, and NIAID says call the provider for mild symptoms within 2 hours.

**Probably *not* an allergy** (ASCIA 2026, Rec 7): a mild rash *only* around the
mouth, with no other symptoms — very common, often just contact irritation from
acidic food. **Offer the food again**; barrier cream before meals helps.
Pulling a food on a false alarm may itself raise allergy risk. Likewise,
gagging or coughing on a new texture is a texture problem, not an immune one.

**FPIES is a different animal** (non-IgE): **delayed, forceful, repetitive
vomiting 1–4 hours** after eating, with pallor and lethargy — no hives, no
wheeze. Antihistamines and epinephrine aren't the treatment (fluids are). The
common triggers are the supposedly-bland first foods: **cow's milk, soy, rice,
oat**. Most children outgrow it by 3–5 years. Worth recognising precisely
because it looks nothing like the allergy we're all watching for.

### The one place to involve a doctor *before* proceeding

**If he has severe eczema** (persistent, needing frequent prescription-strength
steroids despite good emollient use) **or a known egg allergy**, NIAID says
strongly consider peanut-specific IgE or skin-prick testing *before* first
peanut. That group has a meaningfully high chance of already being sensitised —
up to ~30% of infants with moderate-to-severe eczema are already egg-allergic
before 6 months.

Worth knowing there's real disagreement here: **AAAAI/ACAAI/CSACI, ASCIA,
EAACI, and BSACI all say screening is *not* required** even for high-risk
infants, and call it preference-sensitive. Cost-effectiveness modelling favours
no-screening-plus-early-introduction, and BSACI warns that **up to 50% of
positive tests at this age are false positives**, each one risking a needless
elimination diet. What *every* guideline agrees on: **delay is the worst option
on every axis** — cost, quality of life, and reactions.

Also: **don't ask for a broad food allergy panel.** NIAID, AAAAI, and Choosing
Wisely all advise against IgE panels for foods beyond peanut in this context —
poor predictive value, and they generate overdiagnosis and unnecessary
restriction. And being the *sibling* of a food-allergic child does not by itself
raise risk enough to change the plan; BSACI notes it's the *delaying* those
families do that raises risk.

## 3. Iron — the nutrient the first year actually turns on

### Why it's urgent at exactly 6 months

He was born with enough iron to last about six months. Around now those fetal
stores run out, and breast milk contains only **~0.3 mg/L** — nowhere near
enough on its own. The requirement at this age is genuinely startling:

> **11 mg/day of iron for 7–12 months — higher than the RDA for an adult man
> (8 mg).**

That number assumes only ~10% gets absorbed; the true physiological need is
~0.69 mg/day absorbed. And the gap is not theoretical. In US dietary data,
**95.8% of exclusively breastfed 6–12 month infants** were below the absorbed-iron
requirement (54% of all infants that age), with breastfed 6–9 month olds
averaging ~0.27 mg/day absorbed against a ~0.69 mg need.

### Why we care more than "he'll catch up"

Lozoff's Costa Rica cohort is the reason this matters more than most nutrition
worries. Children with moderate iron-deficiency anemia in infancy scored lower
at age 5 *after* controlling for socioeconomic status — and when the same
cohort was retested at **11–14 years**, those with chronic severe iron
deficiency in infancy still showed deficits in arithmetic, written expression,
motor function and spatial memory, with more grade repetition. **All of them had
normal hemoglobin by age 5.** Altered memory-related neural signatures were
still detectable at 10 years.

The honest counterweight, which changes what we do: **supplementation trials
show only modest reversal** — a meta-analysis of 3 RCTs found no significant
effect on the Mental Development Index at 12 months. Iron given after the fact
does not reliably undo it. That's an argument for *prevention through daily
food*, not for supplements-as-insurance.

Equally, **don't maximise.** Excess iron in already-iron-replete infants is
associated with poorer growth, adverse gut microbiota shifts, and intestinal
inflammation. The target is "iron-rich food daily," not "as much as possible."

### What the guidelines say to serve

WHO 2023 makes this one of its few **strong** recommendations: **"Animal source
foods, including meat, fish, or eggs, should be consumed daily"** (Rec 4a) —
because when animal foods were excluded from 6–8 month olds' diets, iron, zinc,
and B12 needs simply could not be met. Canada's CPS is equally direct: the first
complementary foods at 6 months should be iron-rich.

**Heme iron (animal) absorbs at ~25%; non-heme (plant) far lower** — and meat
carries a bonus "meat factor" that boosts absorption of non-heme iron eaten
alongside it. Practically: a spoon of liver or flaked fish stirred into lugaw
does double duty.

**Zinc is the co-limiting nutrient** — the same foods and the same inhibitor
(phytate) limit both, so a low-iron diet is almost always a low-zinc diet. In
the meat-vs-cereal RCT, first-food meat delivered **1.9 mg/day zinc vs 0.6 mg**
from iron-fortified cereal.

Note also that **iron-fortified infant cereal is a North American answer**: in
Philippine data its uptake is **0–6%** across every wealth group. Advice built
on it is culturally mismatched here. And rice cereal specifically shouldn't be
the default — rice takes up ~10× more arsenic than other grains, and the AAP
says there's no evidence supporting it as the standard first food.

### The Philippine picture — and the single most useful number we found

Anemia in Filipino infants **6–11 months** is the worst-hit group in the
country: 2019 ENNS puts it at **~38%** of 6–11 month olds (earlier rounds ran
higher — 48% in 2018, 55.7% in 2008), with a roughly **4× gradient** between the
poorest and wealthiest households.

And the failure is *quality, not timing*: **7 in 10** Filipino children get
complementary food introduced on time, but **9 in 10 are not getting an
adequate diet** and **8 in 10 fail minimum dietary diversity**. Across the diet,
fewer than five foods make up 60–80% of everything eaten.

Which brings us to the "lugaw problem," measured directly in a Philippine study
(mg per 100 g dry weight):

| Porridge | Iron | Zinc | Calcium |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Plain rice lugaw | **0.45** | 1.53 | 5 |
| Rice + **chicken liver** | **10.20** | 5.30 | 11 |
| Rice + **egg yolk** | **4.93** | 4.52 | 138 |
| Rice + **fish** | **2.15** | 3.50 | 663 |
| Rice + **monggo** | **1.53** | 1.93 | 29 |

**Plain lugaw → lugaw with chicken liver is roughly 23× the iron.** Same bowl,
same effort. The same study modelled an ordinary day — egg at breakfast, fish at
lunch, liver at dinner — and got **12.7 mg/day, 116% of a 10-month-old's need**,
entirely from normal household food. No imported product required.

Worth noting: the NNC's own quick guide endorses lugaw and suggests adding milk,
squash, monggo and banana — an energy-and-vitamin list that **doesn't lead with
iron-dense animal foods**, which is a real gap against WHO Rec 4a.

### Iron in foods we actually cook (USDA FoodData Central, per 100 g)

| Food | Iron | Zinc | Vit C | Calcium |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Pork liver**, braised | **17.9 mg** | 6.72 | 23.6 | 10 |
| **Chicken liver**, simmered | **11.6 mg** | 3.98 | 27.9 | 11 |
| Beef liver, braised | 6.54 mg | 5.30 | 1.9 | 6 |
| **Dilis (anchovy)**, canned, drained | **4.63 mg** | 2.44 | 0 | 232 |
| **Malunggay**, raw | **4.0 mg** | 0.60 | **51.7** | 185 |
| Dilis, raw | 3.25 mg | 1.72 | 0 | 147 |
| Beef, ground 85%, cooked | 2.6 mg | 6.31 | 0 | 18 |
| **Monggo**, boiled | 1.4 mg | 0.84 | 1.0 | 27 |
| Egg, hard-boiled | 1.19 mg | 1.05 | 0 | 50 |
| **Bangus (milkfish)**, raw | **0.32 mg** | 0.82 | 0 | 51 |

Three things fall out of this table:

- **Bangus is a poor iron source** — 0.32 mg — despite being the default
  "healthy fish" in Filipino kitchens. It's fine food; it is not an iron
  strategy.
- **Dilis is ~10× better than bangus on iron**, brings serious calcium, is
  cheap, and sits on the **FDA's low-mercury "Best Choices" list**. The cheapest,
  most native option is also the best one here.
- **Malunggay carries its own vitamin C** (51.7 mg) alongside its non-heme iron —
  a self-enhancing pairing. Lugaw + malunggay + dilis is genuinely well-designed.

**Liver caveat:** it's in a different league for iron, but it's also extremely
vitamin-A dense, so it's a few-times-a-week food, not a daily one. Exact
infant frequency limits are worth asking the pediatrician about — we couldn't
find PH-specific guidance.

**Phytate and soaking:** rice, monggo and unrefined grains carry phytate, which
blocks iron and zinc. **Soaking monggo cut phytate 45%** in the Philippine study
(275.6 → 151.0 mg/100 g). Free win — soak them.

**Vitamin C pairing** is real but over-claimed: single-meal studies show 50 mg
vitamin C roughly tripling non-heme iron absorption, but whole-diet studies find
the effect "far less pronounced," ranging from a decrease to 3.5×. Pair
calamansi or papaya with plant iron — just don't treat it as a multiplier we can
bank on.

### The rest of the nutrition picture

- **Vitamin D: 400 IU/day** for breastfed infants (AAP/NAM), from the first days
  of life until weaned onto fortified milk. NHS says 8.5–10 µg. PH-specific
  guidance we couldn't verify — one for the pediatrician.
- **DOH provides free micronutrient powder** for all children 6–23 months (60
  sachets at 6–11 months) — mixed into food *after* cooking. Worth asking about
  at his next check-up rather than buying supplements.
- **No cow's milk as a main drink before 12 months** — very low iron, and it
  causes occult intestinal blood loss in ~40% of infants under one, which is
  itself an anemia risk factor. Yogurt and cheese are fine from ~6–7 months.
- **No honey before 12 months** (infant botulism). **No added salt** — max 1 g/day
  at 6–12 months, and that includes patis, toyo, and bouillon. **No added sugar
  before 2.**
- **Avoid high-mercury fish** — shark, swordfish, king mackerel, marlin, bigeye
  tuna. Salmon, sardines, tilapia, dilis, shrimp and cod are on the Best Choices
  list.

## 4. Raising a kid who eats everything

This is the other half of the question, and it has a clear winner.

### The strongest lever: daily-changing variety in the first two weeks

Not "one new food every three days." **Rotate a different vegetable every
single day.** Maier et al. (2008) compared carrot daily vs 3 vegetables changed
every 3 days vs 3 vegetables changed *daily* — and found **frequency of change
mattered more than the number of foods**.

The follow-up (Maier-Nöth 2016) is the only feeding intervention in the
literature with demonstrated effects *years* later. At **age 6**, the
high-variety group vs low/no variety:

- New-vegetable intake: **14.1 g vs 4.3 and 3.2 g** (p<0.0001)
- New-vegetable liking: **4.4 vs 2.5 and 2.9** (p=0.0002)
- Willingness to taste: **8.9 vegetables vs 5.6** (p=0.0001)
- The initially-*disliked* vegetable was still rated positively by **57%** at 6

Variety also *generalises* — it improves acceptance of genuinely novel foods,
where repeated exposure to a single food only improves that one food (Gerrish &
Mennella 2001). Note the ceiling though: generalisation works **within** a food
category, not across. Vegetable variety buys vegetable acceptance, not fruit.

### Set expectations now: the effect goes invisible around age 2–3

This is the most important thing on this page for our sanity. In that same
study the variety advantage was **undetectable at 15 months (p=0.20) and at 3
years (p=0.06)** — then clearly present again at 6. Food neophobia peaks
between **ages 2 and 6** and is substantially heritable (twin studies put
h² ≈ 0.72–0.78). So when our carefully-broad eater turns two and refuses
everything he loved, that is **normal developmental biology, not our failure,
and not evidence the early work was wasted.** The work is banked, not visible.

Related: **maternal worry about a fussy 15-month-old raised the risk of picky
eating at 38 months from 17% to 50%** (ALSPAC). Anxiety is itself a risk factor.

### How many tries — and where parents give up

Systematic review evidence (USDA/NESR, moderate grade): **8–10+ daily
exposures** to increase acceptance in infants 4–24 months. Surveyed caregivers
give a new food **3–5 tries** before deciding the child dislikes it — roughly a
third of the needed dose. NHS puts it simply: it may take **10 tries or more**.

The popular "8–15 exposures" figure is worth a footnote: it comes from a study
on *preschoolers* eating tofu, not infants, and gets misapplied constantly.

### Textures: the one real deadline

Get **lumpy textures in by 8–10 months.** ALSPAC (n≈7,800–9,400) found children
introduced to lumpy food **after 9 months** had more feeding difficulties and
ate **fewer fruits and vegetables at age 7**; feeding difficulties ran 52% vs
32% comparing late to early. Mechanically this tracks chewing development —
chewing emerges ~8 months and is well established by 10.

Caveat honestly: this is cohort data, not randomised, and reverse causation is
plausible (difficult feeders may have had lumps delayed *because* they were
difficult). But the guidance converges — WHO says lumpy foods 6–9 months;
ESPGHAN says avoid prolonged puréeing, lumpy by 8–10 months at the latest —
and the downside of complying is zero.

Progression from the texture literature: sticky textures from ~8 months, hard
foods ~12 months, raw pieces ~18 months.

### Vegetables before fruit — cheap, probably worth it

The New Zealand RCT (n=117) gave vegetables only vs fruit+vegetables for the
first 4 weeks of solids. At 9 months the veg-only group ate roughly **double**
the broccoli (47 g vs 30 g) and spinach (37 g vs 24 g), and 28% more vegetables
at home. But *liking* ratings didn't differ, and the larger 4-arm Dutch Baby's
First Bites trial (n=246) found **null results** on vegetable intake at 18 and
24 months. Verdict: free, no downside, real but modest — do it, don't count on
it.

### Feeding style: the best-replicated finding is a *don't*

**Pressure to eat backfires, measurably.** In the Generation R cohort, parental
pressure at age 4 independently predicted *more* fussiness at 6. Recalled
childhood pressure — but not childhood picky eating itself — predicts disordered
eating in adulthood. The AHA statement is direct: pressure impairs
self-regulation, and restriction increases eating when not hungry. Recommended
posture is **structure, not control**: consistent meal times and routines, we
decide what and when, he decides whether and how much (Satter's Division of
Responsibility).

Fair warning on that framework: DOR is *consistent with* the strong
"don't pressure" evidence and is endorsed in spirit by WHO, but as a positive
intervention it's thinly evidenced — WHO rates responsive feeding "strong
recommendation, **low** certainty," and reviews note a lack of effectiveness
trials. It rests on solid negative evidence, not proven positive effect.

**What does have positive evidence: modelling.** Meta-analysis puts parental
modelling among the strongest correlates of children's healthy eating (r = .32),
ahead of mere availability (r = .24) — with the specific behaviour being
caregivers visibly and enthusiastically eating the food themselves. Shared
family meals where everyone eats the same food is protective; ready-prepared
foods, and late introduction of lumps and vegetables, are risk factors.

### Two things to skip worrying about

**Baby-led weaning is optional, not decisive.** The BLISS RCT (n=206) found
BLW's advantages modest and partly transient: less fussiness and more food
enjoyment at 12 months, but the **fussiness advantage was gone by 24 months**,
no BMI difference, and — against BLW's own central claim — **lower** satiety
responsiveness at 24 months. On choking: **35% of infants choked at least once
between 6 and 8 months in *both* groups**, with no significant difference — but
that trial included explicit safety coaching and avoidance of hazard-shaped
foods, and the authors noted serious choking events in both arms and
inconsistent supervision. So: BLW-with-safety-discipline isn't riskier, and
it's a reasonable choice, but variety, texture timing, and not pressuring are
the levers that actually move outcomes. (A Metro Manila cross-sectional study
reports a spectacular 95% reduction in picky eating with strict BLW — an odds
ratio of 0.05 from a convenience sample is almost certainly confounded. Ignore
the magnitude.)

**The "6–18 month flavour window" is largely marketing.** The one RCT designed
to test a sensitive period in flavour learning found a much earlier and
narrower one (before ~4 months, for formula flavour specifically). The
defensible version is a **gradual decline in plasticity**, not a door that
slams: children over 24 months respond less to exposure, yet variety at 5–6
months was still measurable at age 6.

### Salt and sugar aren't symmetrical

- **Salt preference is genuinely learned from exposure.** Newborns are
  indifferent to salt; acceptance emerges ~4 months and tracks what they're
  fed. Guidance: no added salt, and that includes **stock cubes, bouillon, patis
  and toyo**. Max **1 g salt/day (0.4 g sodium) at 6–12 months.**
- **Sweet preference is mostly innate and age-graded** — elevated above adult
  levels throughout childhood, declining in adolescence as growth stops. A
  toddler with a sweet tooth is normal biology, not a parenting failure. The
  evidence that early sugar programs a lasting sweet tooth is weak and
  stimulus-specific. The AHA "no added sugar before 2" rule is real, but its
  basis is **displacement and cardiometabolic risk** — tiny calorie budgets
  leave no room for foods that don't carry nutrition — not taste programming.

So: skip added salt because it shapes his palate, and skip added sugar because
it crowds out nutrition. Different reasons, both worth following.

## 5. What this means for the app

The evidence maps almost one-to-one onto the data model in [PLAN.md](PLAN.md),
and it sharpens a few fields:

- **Allergen rotation nudge is the killer feature** — "last served N days ago,"
  weekly target, per allergen. Pure `Date` math over the log.
- **An `ironRich` flag with a *daily* nudge**, not weekly — WHO's animal-source-
  foods-daily recommendation is the one nutrition rule with strong grading, and
  38% local anemia at his age makes it the highest-stakes thing we track. Show
  "no iron-rich food logged today" on the Today screen.
- **Store `ironMg` and `vitCMg` per food** so the reference can flag the
  counterintuitive cases directly on the food card — bangus reading "low iron,
  pair with something else" is worth more than any generic advice.
- **Liver needs a frequency *ceiling*, not a floor** — the only food in the set
  where more is worse (vitamin A). Model it as `maxPerWeek`.
- **Track a `variety` view for the first 2–4 weeks**: distinct vegetables served
  per day, since *frequency of change* is the active ingredient.
- **Exposure counter per food, target 8–10** — with a visible reminder that
  most parents quit at 3–5. This directly counteracts the documented failure.
- **Texture stage on the profile, with an 8–10 month lumpy-food prompt.** The
  one genuine deadline in the literature deserves an actual nudge.
- **Per-food fields the research demands:** `allergen`, `cookedFormRequired`
  (egg), `chokingRisk` + `minAge` for the hard rules (whole nuts <4, no PB
  lumps), `serve` text by age band, and `sources`.
- **A "not a reaction" note** on the reaction-logging flow — mild perioral rash
  alone, and gagging on texture, should not silently become a lifelong
  elimination.
- **Deliberately out of scope:** anything that reads as medical advice or
