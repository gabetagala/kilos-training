## The honest framing, first

**Three 4-week phases are an organising device, not a physiological mechanism.**
Volume-equated meta-analysis puts periodized vs non-periodized hypertrophy at
**ES ≈ 0.13** — negligible. Linear vs undulating: **SMD = 0.02**. For strength
it's better but still modest (ES ≈ 0.31), and only in trained lifters.
([Research Portal](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/effects-of-periodization-on-strength-and-muscle-hypertrophy-in-vo/) ·
[Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/periodization-data/))

So the phases earn their place because they give you **three clean windows for
rotating accessories, stepping volume, and testing** — not because 12 weeks of
steady work would fail. Don't expect the structure itself to grow anything.

**What the structure genuinely buys you** is the thing you actually asked for:
variety that is *audited*, so nothing silently drops below threshold.

## 1. Where your volume actually lands

Counted **fractionally** — a direct set = 1.0, an indirect set = 0.5. This
model beat both total-counting and direct-only counting across 67 studies /
2,058 participants ([Pelland et al. 2025, *Sports Medicine*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/)).
It is the single most useful methodological finding for auditing a program, and
it's why "10 sets of back" and "10 fractional sets of lats" are different claims.

Efficiency tiers for hypertrophy from the same paper (smallest detectable
effect = 2.05%): **<4 undetectable · 5–10 high efficiency · 11–18 intermediate
· 19–29 low · 30+ wasteful.** Marginal return is **0.24% muscle size per
fractional set** at ~12 sets/wk — diminishing, but no plateau found.

| Muscle | Fractional sets/wk | Tier | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forearm | 19.5 | low-eff | **Over-served.** Diminishing returns — don't add more |
| Glutes | 18.0 | intermediate | Good |
| Side delt | 12.0 | intermediate | Good — the taper's headline muscle |
| Biceps | 11.0 | intermediate | Good |
| Hamstrings | 11.0 | intermediate | Good (3 RDL slots doing the work) |
| Chest | 10.0 | high-eff | Fine; rises when the bench lands |
| Upper back | 9.5 | high-eff | Fine |
| Triceps | 9.5 | high-eff | Fine |
| **Lats** | **7.0** | high-eff | **The gap.** Lowest-hanging fix for a V-taper |
| **Quads** | **7.0** | high-eff | **The gap.** Your doc already calls this "a conscious trade" |
| Obliques | 6.0 | high-eff | Fine *(was 3.0 — see the audit note below)* |
| Rear delt | 4.5 | high-eff | Thin but above threshold |
| Front delt | 2.0 | under MEV | **Intentional** — front delts get plenty from pressing; MEV is ~0 |

**The audit already paid for itself.** Folding the suitcase carry into one EMOM
minute (both sides in 60s) cut per-side time under load 80s → 60s and dropped
obliques to **3.0 — under threshold**. Caught, and fixed by splitting the sides
back into their own minutes (3 × 30s/side = 90s, above where it started). That
is exactly the "wasting by not hitting threshold" failure this block is designed
to prevent, and it happened within an hour of the change.

**Block targets:** hold everything ≥ its current tier; bring **lats 7 → 10** and
**quads 7 → 9** by Phase 3. Do not add forearm work.

### The finishers move these numbers — and the first pool broke them

Adding the rotating finishers changed the picture, and the audit caught a real
fault. The first pool had **two chest finishers** (Tabata Push-Up and Death by
Push-Up) among six items, drawn by four days at once — so some weeks served
both and **chest hit 30 fractional sets (the "wasteful" tier)** while other
weeks served neither and it fell to 10. A **3× week-to-week swing** in a
priority muscle is precisely the failure this whole document exists to prevent.

Fixed by making the pool **one item per muscle**, so four distinct day-offsets
into six items can never double up. Death by Push-Up came out; a band pull-apart
went in, because rear delts were the thinnest thing in the program at 4.5.

**Worst-week audit across the full 6-week rotation** (min → max fractional
sets/wk):

| | min | max | | | min | max |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glutes | 22 | 26 | | Triceps | 9.5 | 13.5 |
| Forearm | 19.5 | 24.5 | | Upper back | 9 | 13.5 |
| Chest | 10 | 18 | | Lats | 7 | 17 |
| Biceps | 11 | 16 | | Quads | 7 | 15 |
| Hamstrings | 13 | 13 | | Obliques | 6 | 14 |
| Side delt | 12 | 12 | | Rear delt | 4.5 | 12.5 |

**No muscle falls below MEV in any week**, and nothing reaches the wasteful
line. Front delt sits at 2.0 by design — its MEV is ~0 because pressing
saturates it. Lats and quads still dip to 7 in the weeks their finisher isn't
served; 7 is the efficient-but-modest end of the productive range, not a gap —
but it's why the Phase 2/3 volume steps still matter.

## 2. The three phases

Anchors (**weighted pull-up · front squat · floor press**) run all 12 weeks and
**never rotate.** They carry progressive overload and they are the strength
test. Everything else rotates.

**Why that split is safe:** Baz-Valle 2019 randomised exercise selection *every
single session* for 8 weeks and found **no hypertrophy cost** (VL ES 1.43 vs
1.03 control) — but bench 1RM progressed **+4.7% control vs +0.77% varied**.
Rotation costs you *strength tracking*, not muscle. So rotate everything you
don't measure, and never rotate what you do.
([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0226989))

Two more findings shaping this: Fonseca 2014 — varied-exercise groups grew **all
four quadriceps heads**, constant-exercise groups **missed some**
([JSCR](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2014/11000/changes_in_exercises_are_more_effective_than_in.9.aspx)).
And Kassiano 2022 — *systematic* variation helps, *random* variation hurts
([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/)). So: defined pools, not dice.

There is **no evidence-based rotation cadence.** Four weeks is a design choice.

| | **Phase 1 · wk 1–4 — BUILD** | **Phase 2 · wk 5–8 — PRESS** | **Phase 3 · wk 9–12 — PEAK** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anchors | unchanged | unchanged | unchanged |
| Lateral raise | DB | Cable | Band + DB drop |
| Row slot | 1-arm cable row | Chest-supported DB row | 1-arm cable row |
| Curl | Hammer | Supinated | Reverse |
| Triceps | Rope pushdown | Overhead | Pushdown + overhead |
| Fly | Band | Low cable | Band |
| Carry | Suitcase + farmer | Farmer heavy | Suitcase + farmer |
| **Volume step** | baseline | **+2 lat sets** (add a pulldown minute to "The Spread") | **+2 quad sets** (4th split-squat set) |
| Piece format | EMOM | EMOM + one for-time piece | EMOM + for-time + AMRAP finisher |

**Set progression is the least-evidenced part of this plan.** Enes 2024 — the
only direct 12-week test — found stepped set increases **neutral for
hypertrophy** (CSA p = 0.067) but **clearly better for strength**
([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37796222/)). No study has
volume-matched a ramping group against a fixed one. The +2/+2 steps above are
modest on purpose.

## 3. Deload — a gate, not a date

**Do not schedule one.** Coleman et al. 2024 gave resistance-trained lifters a
1-week deload at the midpoint of a 9-week program: it **negatively affected
lower-body strength**, with no benefit to hypertrophy, power, or endurance
([PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/16777/)). Bell et al. note the risk of
non-functional overreaching from sustained high-volume lifting without deloading
is **low**, and reframe the planned deload as a **checkpoint where you decide**
([Strength & Cond J](https://doras.dcu.ie/31501/1/a_practical_approach_to_deloading__recommendations.203(2).pdf)).

This matches the policy already in TRAINING.md ("reactive only, no calendar") —
which turns out to be the better-supported position.

**Checkpoint at the end of wk 4 and wk 8. Deload only if a trigger fires:**
- You fail to match last session's load × reps on an anchor **two sessions running**, or
- Session RPE climbs while output falls, or
- Any back symptom lasting more than a session.

**If it fires** (Bell's moderate tier): **5–7 days, volume −40–60%, load −10%,
RIR 2–3, frequency unchanged.** Otherwise carry straight on.

## 4. Testing — three tests, three time domains

**One benchmark is not a fitness score.** The evidence is unambiguous: Grace
(~3 min) is **77% explained by whole-body strength** (R² = 0.77); Fight Gone Bad
(17 min) is **59–72% explained by aerobic measures**; and Cindy (20 min) had
**no significant physiological predictors at all** — meaning a 20-minute AMRAP
is not automatically an engine test, it can just be a pacing-and-skill test.
([*Sports* 2020;8(8):112](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7466681/) ·
[*Sci Rep* 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8012697/))

**Retest cadence is genuinely contested.** CrossFit HQ says retest every 1–2
weeks; the widely-repeated "8–12 weeks" figure is **folklore with no source**
that contradicts HQ's own guidance; OPEX argues repeated fixed benchmarks stop
measuring fitness and start measuring learned strategy. The tiebreaker is noise:
**Fight Gone Bad's SEM is 6%**, the only published noise floor for a metcon.
A 4% improvement is measurement error. So a hard test needs a long gap; a
low-noise submaximal test can run monthly.

### TEST A — "THE THREE" · long domain, AMRAP 20 · wk 1, 6, 12

```
AMRAP 20 MINUTES
   5 strict pull-ups
  10 push-ups
  15 box squats
```
**Score: rounds completed.** Cindy's shape, and deliberately so — it's the most
repeatable benchmark there is, and it needs nothing but a bar and a box.

*Honest caveat:* Cindy itself had **no significant physiological predictors** in
the lab (not CrossFit Total, not VO₂max, not Wingate power, not anaerobic
threshold). A 20-minute AMRAP is not automatically an engine test — it's a
pacing and durability test. That's still worth measuring, but it's why it isn't
the only one.

*A note on the format rule:* AMRAP is banned from carrying **programmed
volume**, because a dose of "whatever you managed" can't be audited. A **test**
isn't delivering a dose — it's producing a score, and AMRAP produces a clean
scalar. Different job, different rule.

### TEST B — "DESCENT" · short domain, for time · wk 1, 6, 12

```
21-15-9 FOR TIME
  box squats
  push-ups
```
**Score: total time**, expect 4–6 min. A genuinely different quality from Test
A — Grace (~3 min) is 77% explained by strength while Fight Gone Bad (17 min) is
59–72% aerobic, which are near-orthogonal. Improving one tells you almost
nothing about the other.

### TEST C — "THE CONTROL" · submaximal · every 4 weeks (wk 1, 4, 8, 12)

3 minutes of step-ups at a metronome-fixed cadence with a fixed pack, then stand
still and record **heart rate at 60 s post (HRR60)**.

Grounded in the YMCA/Queen's College step tests (reliability 0.92). **Do not use
the published VO₂max formulas** — your box height won't match theirs, so the
normative equations don't apply. This is a **within-person tracker only**: same
box, same cadence, same pack, same time of day. Improving HRR60 at identical
work is a clean, low-noise read on cardiac fitness, and unlike a max test it
isn't confounded by how much grit you had that morning.

**This is the only test sensitive enough to show a change inside four weeks.**
A and B need 8–12 weeks to beat their own noise. C catches the trend; A and B
confirm it.

### Why every movement here is safe to max on

Strict pull-up (a hanging decompression, and **never kipped** — kipping is rapid
lumbar flexion/extension cycling) · push-up (fails at the chest, hips sag into
*extension*) · **box** squat (fatigue drives posterior pelvic tilt at the bottom
of a free air squat; a fixed box defines depth mechanically, so rep 200 is the
same rep as rep 1) · box step-up (upright torso throughout).

**There is no rep in any of these where fatigue produces loaded lumbar
flexion.** That's the whole design — a test you can't safely max on isn't a test.

### Strength testing

Don't retest true maxes mid-block — 1RM CV is ~2–4%, so a real change needs to
clear **~5%**, and four weeks of trained progress usually won't.
([systematic review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7367986/))
Instead, `bestE1RM()` in [progression.js](src/workout/progression.js#L43) already
computes estimated 1RM from every logged set — read that **weekly as a trend**,
and retest the three anchors properly at **wk 12**.

### Protocol rules that shrink noise (all three tests)

Same time of day · same warm-up · **≥48 h from a hard session** (CrossFit
recovery runs 48–72 h) · identical equipment and standards · **two
familiarisation runs before the wk-1 baseline**, because the learning effect on a
novel conditioning test is large and will fake a gain.

## 5. The morning rule, corrected

Your current rule says no heavy axial or hinge loading within ~2 h of waking.
The evidence refines it usefully: **the morning effect is about *bending*, not
load.** Overnight the discs superhydrate, which makes them **stiffer in
compression** but **more flexible and more vulnerable in bending** — lumbar
flexion ROM rises ~5° from morning to afternoon, and bending stresses are
commonly cited as ~3× higher early
([Adams, Dolan & Hutton 1987, *Spine*](https://journals.lww.com/spinejournal/abstract/1987/03000/diurnal_variations_in_the_stresses_on_the_lumbar.8.aspx)).
Snook's 18-month RCT found controlling early-morning lumbar flexion cut pain days
**23% vs 2%** in controls, and **51% further** at 3-year follow-up among those
who kept it up ([Snook et al. 1998](https://kdttechnique.com/the-reduction-of-clbp-through-control-of-early-morning-flexion-snook-sh-et-al-spine-1998-dec/)).
That is one of very few genuinely RCT-backed spine-hygiene interventions.

**So:** the rule is *"don't bend the loaded spine in the morning,"* not *"don't
load it."*

- **Fine first thing:** carries, step-ups, push-ups, walking, cycling, the whole
  Tier-1 menu. Axial compression on a *neutral* spine is exactly what the morning
  disc handles best. **All three benchmark tests are morning-safe.**
- **Wants ~60 min upright first:** the RDL slots (A1, B2, C1) and the B1 front
  squat.

This makes morning conditioning + later lifting a genuinely well-motivated split.

## 6. Conditioning budget — how much is safe

Better news than expected. Two 2022/2026 meta-analyses put concurrent training's
cost to **hypertrophy at SMD −0.01** and **maximal strength at −0.06** — i.e.
nil ([Schumann et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/) ·
[umbrella review 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41762427/)).

**The one real cost is explosive strength: SMD −0.28**, concentrated in
same-session pairing (≤20 min apart: −0.31; separated by ≥3 h: no significant
effect). **That flags your Power Primer**, which opens A2 and C2 directly before
a metcon. Mitigations, in order: it already runs **first and fresh**, which is
the protective order; if the primer's numbers stall across the block, move it to
Sunday rather than shrinking it.

Duration is a more dangerous knob than frequency (r up to −0.75 vs −0.35), so
prefer more, shorter bouts — which is also McGill's own capacity model
(*"if you will have pain on a 40-minute walk, just walk 20 a couple of times per
day"*). Your 20–25 min pieces sit comfortably under every threshold in the
literature.

## 7. Movement rulings for this block

Carried over from the DDD research, in case anything tempts you mid-block.

**Never, in any format:** thrusters · high-rep deadlifts · GHD sit-ups ·
toes-to-bar · wall balls. All are loaded or dynamic lumbar flexion, and every one
gets worse as a function of fatigue.

**Rowing is out — and it's the surprise.** Peak L4/L5 compression **~4.6×
bodyweight**; the erg produces *more* lumbar flexion than on-water rowing; and
fatigue increases flexion at the catch, more so on the erg. Erg volume and prior
lumbar injury are the two strongest predictors of back pain in rowers
([systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33397675/)). **Do not buy
a rower.** I recommended one earlier in the conversation; that was wrong.

**KB swings need more caution than "wait a quiet month."** McGill & Marshall
measured a 16 kg swing at ~3200 N — modest — but it creates **posterior shear of
L4 on L5, opposite in polarity to a normal lift**, so the facets are in tension
and can't share load. McGill: *"may be contraindicated for some individuals with
spine shear load intolerance."* Introduce single-arm-light and stop at any
symptom ([JSCR 2012;26(1):16-27](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2012/01000/kettlebell_swing,_snatch,_and_bottoms_up_carry_.3.aspx)).

**Jump rope is still a green light** — axial impact, spine vertical, minimal
flexion. Belavý 2017 found runners had *better* disc hydration and proteoglycan
content than non-athletes.

**Air squats: use a box.** Fatigue drives posterior pelvic tilt at the bottom;
a fixed box-touch defines depth and mechanically prevents the tuck, turning a
risky-when-fatigued movement into a safe one.

**Carry hierarchy, measured** ([McGill, Marshall & Andersen 2013, *Ergonomics*](https://www.backfitpro.com/medical-scientific-articles/2013/)):
30 kg in **each** hand = **2339 N** · 30 kg in **one** hand = **2874 N** ·
NIOSH action limit = 3400 N. Double-hand carries are the ones to load; suitcase
carries stay deliberately light and short.

### The honest counter-evidence

McGill's model has credentialed opposition and you should know it.
[Saraceni et al. 2020, *JOSPT*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31775556/) —
systematic review + meta-analysis — found **low-quality evidence that greater
lumbar flexion during lifting is NOT a risk factor** for low-back-pain onset or
persistence. Seven cross-sectional studies found people *with* pain lifted with
**6.0° less** flexion. And McGill's disc-failure mechanism rests largely on
**porcine and cadaveric tissue** — dead, non-adapting material cycled to failure.

The population-level claim ("everyone should avoid flexion") is weakly
supported. The claim this block is built on — **"if flexion provokes *your*
symptoms, removing loaded and fatigued flexion reduces *your* pain"** — is
supported by both the mechanism and the Snook RCT. It's a subgroup argument, and
you're the subgroup. McGill also refuses to give a dose: *"There is not a single
number – it is a variable."* Anyone quoting you a rep limit for flexion is
inventing it.

**Built and runnable (2026-08-07).** All three benchmarks are live in the app

- **"The Three"** runs on the `amrap` mode; **"Descent"** on `fortime` with a

- **The noise band is enforced in code** ([benchmark.js](src/workout/benchmark.js)).
  A change inside the band renders as `=`, never as an improvement: a 4% faster
  Descent shows flat, a +40% Three shows `▲40%`. Telling you that you improved
  when you didn't is worse than telling you nothing.

**Finishers (2026-08-07).** Four days — Mon, Thu, Fri, Sat — end on a short,
fun, bodyweight piece drawn from a **six-deep rotating pool**: Tabata Push-Up ·
Tabata Squat · Death by Push-Up · The Grip (AMRAP farmer carry) · Tabata Side
Plank · The Ladder (strict pull-up ladder). The pool advances once per completed
run of that session, so it's a different finisher every week, cycling — and each
day starts at a different offset so Monday and Thursday never serve the same one.

This is the one slot where the volume-accounting rules are **deliberately off**:
a finisher carries no muscle's weekly dose, so it's free to use formats whose
volume isn't auditable. The safety rule is not off — every movement still fails
at the lungs, legs, chest or grip, never at a flexing spine. No burpees: highest
flexion-cycle count per minute in the canon.

Day lengths across the whole rotation: **31–42 min all in**, inside the 45-min
ceiling. The estimate is pessimistic for the ladder finishers — it counts their
full queue ceiling, but a death-by ends at failure, so a day reading 42 really
runs ~35.

**Still not built: block/week state.** Nothing in the app knows which of the 12
weeks you're in, so the **phase accessory rotations and the test calendar are
manual** — you'll need to swap the Phase 2/3 exercise pools by hand and remember
which weeks are test weeks. That's the next piece.
